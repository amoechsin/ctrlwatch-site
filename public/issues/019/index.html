▌ EDITOR'S LETTER ▌

Everyone Is Keeping Score

This magazine ranks fifty channels an issue, scores five categories to two digits, and prints a table with movement arrows. We are in no position to pretend we're above keeping score. So this issue we stopped pretending and went to look at the scoreboard itself — at what happened when actual competition, the kind with winners, moved onto a platform that was already one.

YouTube is a scoreboard-shaped medium. It always was. Every video ships with its result stapled underneath it in public — views, likes, the brutal arithmetic of the subscriber counter — and every creator on the platform is in a ranked match whether they entered one or not. The algorithm does not have taste; it has standings. Which makes it strange, when you think about it, how long it took us to do this issue: a magazine obsessed with rankings, on a platform that is one, has never once looked squarely at the people whose actual subject is competition. The speedrunners. The chess people. The breakdown artists. The trick-shot industrialists. The man in Spain who invented a boxing league out of nothing and broke the concurrent-viewership record for the entire internet doing it.

And here is what we found, and it is genuinely the opposite of what the money believed. For most of the last decade, the smartest capital in entertainment was betting that competition on the internet would look like television — franchised leagues, city teams, ten-million-dollar slots, a Sunday broadcast with a desk. That bet is now a smoking crater, and our Special Feature walks the whole debris field: the Overwatch League voting itself out of existence, franchise slots bought for $33 million and sold for $10 million, a $725 million esports company delisting its way to a fire sale. The esports winter was real. But the viewers never left — they just walked across the street to where a streamer was organising a boxing night, and nine million of them watched it at once.

Competition didn't die on YouTube. The attempt to make it television did. What survived is older than television: somebody sets a challenge, somebody swears they can beat it, and everybody crowds round to watch.

That's the thesis, and every section this issue tests it from a different corner. Our Player Profiles run the full spread: the return of Summoning Salt, whose speedrun histories we dropped from the Top 50 in a displacement three years of work has made indefensible; Jomboy Media, the lip-reading baseball desk that a ninety-billion-dollar league eventually decided to buy into rather than out-compete; Ibai Llanos, who invents competitions the way other channels invent thumbnails; and Dude Perfect, where this issue's negative review asks the uncomfortable question of what remains of a competition when the losing has been edited out. The Boss Fight is chess — the archivist who kept the game's flame alight against the broadcaster who caught fire when the boom came. Time Capsule opens a new shelf entirely: five people who lived and died by the scoreboard, from Pelé to Bobby Fischer, shown what became of the arena. And Yob, at last, pays a debt the letters page has owed a reader in Birmingham since Issue #016.

One honesty before you start. A competition issue written by a magazine that publishes a Top 50 is a mirror held up to a mirror, and we know it. When we say Dude Perfect removed the stakes, someone will say our rankings have none either — nobody gets relegated from YouTube for losing our table. Fair. The difference, we'd argue, is the one this whole issue keeps finding: a real competition is legible. You can see the attempts, the failures, the record before this one. Our scores print their working; the reviews are the attempts, in public, on the record, arguable. That is the only kind of authority this magazine has ever claimed — the kind you can check. The scoreboard is open. Argue with it.

— The Editor

▶ THE FEATURE IS RELEVANT
Riot Kills the LTA After One Season — the League Model Can't Stop Reorganising Itself

In June 2024 Riot announced that three regional League of Legends leagues — the LCS, Brazil's CBLOL, and Latin America's LLA — would merge into one pan-American super-league, the LTA, launching 2025. On September 28, 2025, Riot discontinued the LTA after exactly one season, reviving the standalone LCS and CBLOL for 2026 and citing what fans had said from day one: you cannot merge away regional pride and call it strategy. Read that timeline again. The premier esport on Earth restructured its American operation, watched it fail, and restructured it back, inside eighteen months. Our Special Feature this issue is about the decade-long unravelling of the franchised-league dream, and the LTA is its perfect miniature — a model so unsure of itself it now reorganises faster than its audience can learn the new acronym. The games are fine. The viewers are fine. The org chart is the thing that keeps dying.

▶ THE COUNTER-TREND
La Velada VI Goes Tri-Platform — Ibai's Boxing Night Is Now Infrastructure

On July 25, 2026, Ibai Llanos ran the sixth edition of La Velada del Año, and for the first time simulcast it across Twitch, YouTube, and TikTok — with reported viewership north of thirty million devices across the three platforms (the organisers' own aggregate claims run far higher; we cite the platform-split figure because it is the one you can actually reason about). For context: edition five, in 2025, peaked above nine million concurrent viewers on a single channel — the biggest single-channel livestream ever recorded, on any platform, for anything. A streamer's amateur boxing night now outdraws most professional sport in Europe. We review the man responsible in Player Profiles this issue, and the feature explains why his invented competitions thrived through the exact years the franchised leagues were liquidating. Short version: he never once tried to be television.

▶ THE COUNTEREXAMPLE
AGDQ 2026 Raises $2.4M — Speedrunning Declines to Have a Winter

Awesome Games Done Quick 2026 closed in January having raised $2,443,414 for the Prevent Cancer Foundation — following SGDQ 2025's $2,436,614 for Doctors Without Borders. Steady, unglamorous, metronomic: roughly two and a half million dollars per event, twice a year, going on two decades of marathon. Here is the thing worth sitting with. Speedrunning has no franchise slots, no city teams, no venture capital, and no league office — and it is the one corner of competitive gaming that has never had a crash, because there is nothing financialised enough to crash. The couch, the timer, the donation counter. While the feature this issue counts the bodies from the esports winter, hold this number in the other hand: the most durable competitive institution on the platform is a charity marathon run by volunteers. It was never a business. That turns out to be the moat.

▶ THE LEAGUE BUYS THE CREATOR
MLB Takes an Equity Stake in Jomboy Media — the Scoreboard Buys the Commentary

In mid-2025, Major League Baseball took a strategic equity stake in Jomboy Media — by most accounts the first time the league has put actual ownership money into a creator company, after a negotiation Jimmy O'Brien has said took four years and started from terms he called a non-starter. Sit with the shape of that story. In 2019, O'Brien was a guy with editing software who proved the Astros were banging a trash can while the sport's official media apparatus was still clearing its throat. Six years later the league concluded that the correct response to independent scrutiny it couldn't control was to buy a piece of it. We profile Jomboy this issue — the review takes the deal seriously as both a triumph and a question. When the institution you fact-check becomes your shareholder, the fact-checking has a new conflict to disclose. The breakdowns are still great. The cap table got complicated.

▶ SCALE CHECK
The Esports World Cup Paid Out $71.5M Last Summer — the Winter Has a Space Heater, and It's Sovereign

The 2025 Esports World Cup ran in Riyadh from July to August with a $71.5 million prize pool across 24 games — up ten million on 2024's edition, which was already the largest in esports history. If the franchised-league era is dead, somebody forgot to tell the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, whose Public Investment Fund has poured a reported $38 billion into gaming and esports through Savvy Games Group — which also now operates the circuit that replaced the Overwatch League. This is the complicating fact our feature refuses to look away from: the league model didn't just collapse into creator spectacle, it also consolidated into sovereign-wealth spectacle, and "the prize pool is the message" is a sentence with more than one meaning. The winter is real. So is the space heater. Who's paying for the heating, and why, is a question esports has mostly decided not to ask out loud.

Time Capsule

Five people who lived and died by the scoreboard, shown what became of the arena. This issue opens the athletes-and-arena-voices shelf: the most joyful footballer who ever lived, the chess champion who threw the cameras out, the man who invented the sporting brag, the driver who narrated the limit, and the broadcaster who told it like it was before "it" became a format. They see YouTube from inside their own eras. The irony does the rest.

⚠ SATIRICAL / FICTIONAL — Pelé did not participate in this Q&A.
Pelé
Edson Arantes do Nascimento · three-time World Cup winner · responding from Santos, 1970, weeks after Mexico
C+W
We want to show you how people watch football now. Millions of them, on small screens, whenever they like. Start anywhere.
PELÉ
[he has been watching for some time already, smiling the whole while] This one has watched my header against Italy two hundred times, it says. Two hundred! [laughs, delighted] In Brazil, if you missed the match, you missed it. Maybe the radio told you. Maybe a man in the café stood up and performed the goal with his hat. Now the goal lives in a little box and you visit it like a friend. I think this is beautiful. I scored it once. He has seen it two hundred times. Who owns that goal now, him or Pelé? [waves his own question away, grinning] Him. Let him have it.
C+W
There are entire channels of nothing but skills. Ten minutes of stepovers, rainbow flicks, nutmegs — no matches, just the tricks.
PELÉ
Show me. [watches a freestyle compilation in complete silence, leaning closer and closer] The boy in this one — the ball never touches the ground. Never! Where does he play? [hears the answer: he doesn't, he films in a car park] He doesn't play? [sits back] Ah. So this is a new position. In my time, we had this player too — every favela had one, the boy who could do everything with the ball and nothing with the game. We loved him at parties. We did not pass to him. [thinks better of it] But no — I am being unfair, and I promised Dondinho I would never be unfair about skill. What that boy does is real. It is not football, but it is real. It is the joy with the match removed. Half of the thing. The beautiful half, maybe. But which half made it beautiful, eh? That is the question your little box has decided not to ask.
C+W
Here's a channel that analyses tactics. Arrows, diagrams, why the full-back inverted. Twenty minutes on one match's structure.
PELÉ
[watches with polite attention that slowly becomes genuine attention] He is not wrong, this professor. That is more or less what Zagallo tells us, with worse drawings. [tapping the screen] But look what is missing from his arrows. Fear. The arrow does not show you that the full-back is playing with a bad ankle and a worse marriage. Football looks like geometry from up there. From the pitch it is biography — twenty-two biographies, running. [softening] Still. A boy who cannot afford a ticket can learn the game's whole shape from this man for nothing. In Três Corações we learned by watching the ball leave. This is better. I only ask the professor to remember, once per video, that the diagram sweats.
C+W
Something harder. Most people now see football only as highlights. The goal, the skill, the red card. Almost nobody watches the whole ninety minutes.
PELÉ
[the smile stays, but something behind it goes still] Then they have the fruit without the tree. [quiet for a moment] You know what the goal against Italy was? It was Rivelino running for eighty minutes so their midfield was tired in the eighty-sixth. It was Gérson's cigarette calm. The goal is the last sentence of a long story, and your box teaches people to read only last sentences. [then, unable to help himself, the grin returns] Although — I confess something shameful. The last sentences they chose? They chose well. Whoever cuts these highlights loves football. He just loves it the way you love a woman in photographs.
C+W
One more. A twelve-year-old in Jakarta, filming himself practising your bicycle kick against a wall, four hundred attempts, and posting the one that worked.
PELÉ
[watches all four hundred attempts — the compilation shows the failures — and does not speak until it ends] He shows the failures. [moved, and not hiding it] This is the part your box does that nothing in my world does. When I was twelve I practised the bicycle on the sand at Santos and nobody saw the thousand bad ones, so when the good one came, they called it magic. It was not magic. It was the thousand. This boy — he publishes the thousand. He is more honest about skill than my whole legend is. [laughs suddenly] Don't print that. Print it.
C+W
Last question. If this box had existed in 1958 — you, seventeen, in Sweden — what would have happened?
PELÉ
[a long, happy exhale, the answer arriving before the question finished] The world would have seen a black boy from Bauru cry after the final, that same night, and fall in love with him three years earlier than it did. That is the good. The bad — [holds up a finger] — the bad is that seventeen-year-old Pelé would have read what the box said about him at halftime. And I do not know if the boy scores the second goal after reading the comments about the first.
"You have built a stadium where the whole world has a seat, and the whole world has a whistle. The game is still beautiful. But tell your players to score first, and read later — because joy cannot hear the crowd and the comments at the same time."
⚠ SATIRICAL / FICTIONAL — Bobby Fischer did not participate in this Q&A.
Bobby Fischer
1943–2008 · World Chess Champion · responding from Reykjavik, September 1972, days after taking the title
C+W
You just spent a summer fighting, among other things, television cameras. You demanded they be removed from the hall. We'd like to show you what chess looks like fifty years on.
BOBBY FISCHER
[arms folded, already suspicious of the screen itself] If there's a camera in it, I already know what happened. The camera wins. [watches anyway — a grandmaster streaming blitz to an audience of eighty thousand] Wait. [leans in despite himself] He's playing. While he talks. And that number — that's people watching? Eighty thousand people watching a chess game that isn't even a real game, it's five-minute chess, it's — [stops. recalculates.] Eighty thousand. We got three thousand in the hall here and they called it the match of the century.
C+W
After a television show about chess in 2020, the biggest chess website was signing up a million new players a month. It now has more than two hundred million accounts.
BOBBY FISCHER
[a rare, unguarded pause] Two hundred million. [something in the voice that is almost hunger] Do you know what I was doing this summer? Fighting a federation for prize money, because chess players got paid like coat-check clerks and I was the only one rude enough to say the game was worth more. They hated me for the demands. Every player who eats from that number you just said — every one — eats because somebody was rude first. [flat again, catching himself] I'm glad the game got big. I notice it got big from a television show and not from the games. That tells you what people actually watched. They didn't fall in love with chess. They fell in love with somebody winning.
C+W
The most popular chess videos aren't games at all, exactly. They're a commentator recapping tournaments — jokes, drama, storylines. Millions of viewers who never play a rated game.
BOBBY FISCHER
[watching a recap with an expression of deepening offence] He talked for eleven minutes and showed four moves. [jabs at the screen] Where's the game? The game is the interesting part! Twenty-four moves of theory and then a man finds one move nobody in a hundred years found, and this — this announcer — gives it four seconds and a sound effect. [pacing now] This is what I threw the cameras out for. Not because I hate people watching. Because the camera doesn't watch the board. The camera watches the faces. It turns the fight into a soap opera, and eventually the soap opera pays better than the fight, and then God help you, because that's when the players start performing instead of playing.
C+W
You should know it goes further. There are machines now that play far beyond any human. Every viewer watches with a machine's evaluation on screen. Every strong player is suspected, sometimes accused, of consulting one. There was a scandal about it that reached the newspapers of the whole world.
BOBBY FISCHER
[goes very still — the stillness of a man hearing his own private weather confirmed by a forecast] So it finally happened. Everyone laughed at me for the demands — seal the hall, check the chairs, the lights, the cameras. They said Bobby sees enemies everywhere. [a short, bitter laugh] And fifty years later every player in the world sits down across from a stranger and wonders. You built an arena where the strongest player in history is a machine in the audience, and now no victory is clean unless a referee checks your shoes. [quieter] I don't feel vindicated. Understand that. I wanted to be wrong. Suspicion eats the game from inside — I know exactly what it eats first, and it is not the game.
C+W
And yet — the paradox is that more humans play chess now than at any moment in history. A teenager just became world champion. Children study openings free that you had to fight for Russian journals to see.
BOBBY FISCHER
[the arms unfold, slowly] That part is good. [grudging, then less grudging] No — that part is very good, and I'll say it plainly for once. I learned from a dollar chess book and whatever Russian theory I could smuggle into Brooklyn, and the Soviets had forty coaches to my zero, and the whole point of the fight this summer was one person against a system that hoarded the game. If some kid in Lagos or Manila gets the whole library free — every game ever played, every idea — then the system lost and the kid won, permanently. [a warning finger] But tell the kid this from me. The library will make him knowledgeable. Only the board will make him honest.
C+W
Last one. The champion who came after your era says the title brought him mostly pressure — that the fame is a machine you can't switch off. You'd know something about that. Any advice for the ones living it in public now, on camera, every day?
BOBBY FISCHER
[a long silence — long enough to be uncomfortable, which he does not appear to notice] They chose the camera. I need you to understand how strange that is to me. I gave up everything for the board and they are giving up the board for the audience, one recap at a time, and the terrible thing is I can't even tell them they're wrong, because the audience pays and the board never did. [stands, straightening the pieces on a board that was already straight] Advice. Fine.
"The board is the only place in the world where the truth can't be argued with. Sixty-four squares, no cameras, no committee, no crowd. Guard one place in your life like that. I guarded it with everything I had — and you should ask what it cost me, but you should also ask what it's costing you not to have one."
⚠ SATIRICAL / FICTIONAL — Babe Ruth did not participate in this Q&A.
Babe Ruth
1895–1948 · "The Sultan of Swat" · responding from New York, 1930, the year he out-earned the President
C+W
Mr. Ruth, we're going to show you the future of sports entertainment. Fair warning: you may recognise your own fingerprints on it.
BABE RUTH
[cigar going, entirely at ease before the screen is even on] Kid, I invented the future of sports entertainment on a Tuesday and hit two home runs the same afternoon. Show me. [watches a trick-shot video — a basketball dropped from a tower, a bowling-ball flip, five men in matching shirts celebrating] Ha! HA! [slaps his knee, genuinely thrilled] Now that's the stuff. That's the circus half of the business, and don't let anybody tell you the circus half don't count. I used to call my shot — point the bat at the bleachers, you know, give the people something to argue about for thirty years. These boys point at the impossible thing and then film themselves doing it. Same church, kid. Same church.
C+W
We should be straight with you: they film hundreds of attempts and show you the one that lands.
BABE RUTH
[completely unbothered] Sure. And you think I told reporters about the strikeouts? [jabbing the cigar for emphasis] I led the league in strikeouts more years than I care to count, and you know what people remember? The sixty. The called shot. Listen — the brag is a sport too. Saying it, then doing it, that's the whole American religion right there. [then, squinting at the screen with the first flicker of doubt] Only — hold on. Run that again. [watches closer] Where's the pitcher?
C+W
What do you mean?
BABE RUTH
The pitcher, kid. The other guy. The fella whose whole job is making me look like a bum. [gesturing at the trick-shot men] These boys are playing against a tower and a bucket. The tower ain't trying. When I pointed at those bleachers, Charlie Root was sixty feet away wanting to knock my head off, and THAT'S why they still argue about it — because I could've ended up on my backside in front of forty thousand people. No pitcher, no called shot. Just a fella doing tricks. [settling back, cigar relit, judgement rendered] It's good circus. But the brag only counts when somebody's trying to make a liar out of you.
C+W
Here's something else. Athletes now speak directly to the public — no reporters in between. They film their own lives, their training, their breakfasts.
BABE RUTH
[sitting up, doing the arithmetic of it instantly] No writers? None? [a low whistle] Kid, do you know what I'd have given for that. Half of what America knows about me got invented by fellas on deadline — some of it I did, some of it I said I did, some of it Christy Walsh cooked up while I was asleep, and I'm the only man alive who can't remember which is which. [warming to it enormously] A camera of my own! The Babe, direct to the kids, no middleman! I'd have been bigger than — [pauses, honestly consults himself] — well. Bigger than me, and there wasn't room. [quieter, an unexpected note] Though I'll tell you the one thing the writers did that your camera won't. They went home at night. A fella needs some hours that don't have an audience, even a fella like me. Especially a fella like me.
C+W
The five men in matching shirts you liked — their company is worth over a hundred million dollars. They have a chief executive from the basketball league, and they're building a theme park.
BABE RUTH
[a huge laugh that ends sooner than it should] A hundred million! And people said MY salary was a scandal — eighty grand, and I had to explain why I made more than Hoover! I said I had a better year than him, which was true, and also the funniest thing any ballplayer ever said, and I'm including everything said since. [then the cigar comes down, and the face does something more thoughtful than it has all interview] A chief executive, though. Huh. Kid, I've met the executives. Fine fellas, good suits. But nothing with an executive in the room ever stayed fun for long, and the fun was the product. You go ask those five boys in the matching shirts whether they still laugh when the camera's off. Don't ask 'em on camera. You'll get the wrong answer.
C+W
Last question, Babe. A hundred years from your time, kids will watch you swing on this machine — grainy, jerky film, over and over. What do you want them to see?
BABE RUTH
[for once, no performance at all — he looks at the screen the way a man looks out a train window] St. Mary's Industrial School had one thing going for it, kid: nobody watching. A fat orphan nobody wanted, hitting a dead ball against a wall ten thousand times because it was the only thing that felt like being somebody. Every swing you got on that film of yours got built in a yard where there wasn't a single camera, and that's the part the film can't show. [the grin comes back, full wattage] But sure — let 'em watch the swing.
"Tell your boys with the cameras: the pointing at the bleachers, anybody can film. It's the pitcher trying to make a liar out of you that makes it a sport — and if there's no pitcher in the frame, kid, it ain't a brag. It's just talking."
⚠ SATIRICAL / FICTIONAL — Ayrton Senna did not participate in this Q&A.
Ayrton Senna
1960–1994 · three-time Formula One World Champion · responding from São Paulo, winter 1993
C+W
You know the onboard camera — Formula One has carried them for years now. We want to show you what the onboard became.
AYRTON SENNA
[he watches an onboard lap in silence and does not blink for most of it] The quality. You can see everything now — the hands, the small corrections, the moment the rear goes light over the crest. [softly, almost to himself] For years I tried to explain to journalists what happens in Eau Rouge, and they wrote down "he is brave," which is not what happens in Eau Rouge. This camera explains it better than I ever did. The steering wheel is never still. People think we hold a line. There is no line. There is a conversation, two hundred times a second, and now — [gestures at the screen, something opening in his face] — now everyone can watch the conversation.
C+W
There are channels devoted to explaining exactly that. Telemetry traces, braking points, two drivers' inputs overlaid — the kind of data only your engineers see, now narrated for millions.
AYRTON SENNA
[leaning forward now, fully engaged, a finger tracing the telemetry trace on the screen] This is Honda's data screen. On the internet. For everyone. [a small, disbelieving laugh] Do you understand what you are showing me? The most secret room in my world — the room where we argue over three hundredths in the second sector — and you have given the whole world a seat in it. In 1988 I did a lap in Monaco where the car and I went somewhere I still do not have words for, and only the telemetry knew the truth of it. If this had existed then, a million people could have seen what I could not say. [sitting back] This is the best thing you have shown any of us, I think. It does not make the driving smaller, like television does. It makes it bigger. It shows the work inside the miracle.
C+W
The overtake compilations are some of the most-watched racing videos. Your own moves — Donington's first lap, especially — replayed millions of times.
AYRTON SENNA
[watches his own first lap at Donington with a critical stillness, as if scoring it] They always show that lap in the rain and call it magic, and I will tell you the same thing I tell everyone: it is not magic, it is that I decided before Redgate what the others were still deciding at Redgate. The lap was won in the decision, not in the water. [then, noticing something] But look how they cut it. Move, move, move, five overtakes in forty seconds. The lap took a minute and a half. Even here, even for the best lap of my life, ninety seconds is too long for them. [a wry look] What do they do with the parts of life that take years?
C+W
This is the part we hesitated to show you. The crash compilations. Millions of views. Accidents — some of them fatal, from your era and before — watched as entertainment, replayed, slowed down, set to music.
AYRTON SENNA
[he watches perhaps ten seconds, then reaches over and stops it himself — the first time he has touched the machine] No. [a long silence; when he speaks again the voice is level and very quiet] I have stopped my car in the middle of a session and run across a live track to hold a man's head — Comas, at Spa, last year. When Ratzenberger — no. You said this is 1993 for me, so let me say it generally. When we lose someone, we lose someone. It is not footage. [looking directly at the interviewer now] I understand why people watch. The limit is the most honest place in the world, and death standing near it is what makes it the limit — I have said this myself, motor racing is that danger or it is nothing special. But there is a difference between witnessing and consuming. The man in the car has a mother who also has this machine of yours. Set it to music? [shakes his head once] Some things you may watch only if you are willing to pray after.
C+W
A gentler one. Sim racing — ordinary people race your circuits in machines at home, in leagues, seriously. Some have become real racing drivers that way.
AYRTON SENNA
[brightening, and openly curious] This I like, and I will tell you why with a memory. Kart. Everything true I know, I learned in kart — a boy in Interlagos with no money for Formula Ford yet, just laps and laps. Kart was our simulator; it simply also bruised. [watching a sim racer's hands] His hands are doing real things. The fear is missing, and the fear is not nothing — the fear is information. But the craft, the judgement, the racecraft of managing another man's ambition into turn one — this transfers. A poor boy in Brazil who cannot afford one kart season can now do ten thousand laps of Monaco in his bedroom. [nodding slowly] Racing was always a rich man's door. You have built a window. Good.
C+W
Last question, Ayrton. All of it together — the onboards, the data, the millions watching everything. Is something lost?
AYRTON SENNA
[he takes a long time, and it is clearly not for effect] When I am in the car, there is a place I go where there is no crowd. No camera, no championship, not even Ayrton, really — just the next corner arriving, and God very close. That place cannot be broadcast. You can film everything around it — the hands, the data, the eyes — and the cameras now film more of the around than I dreamed possible, and I am glad. [a small smile] But do not let the millions who watch believe they have been there. The onboard shows the conversation. It does not show who I am talking to.
"You can show them my hands, my data, even my eyes — and you should, it is the work inside the miracle. But the limit itself has no camera angle. It is between the driver and God, and it does not stream."
⚠ SATIRICAL / FICTIONAL — Howard Cosell did not participate in this Q&A.
Howard Cosell
1918–1995 · broadcaster, "telling it like it is" · responding from New York, 1975, at the height of Monday Night Football
C+W

Nobody on this platform — nobody — documents competition itself better than a man you almost never see, narrating the decades-long wars over hundredths of a second in games older than most of the people fighting them.

The format has not changed, which is precisely the point; it has deepened. A World Record Progression video takes one game — Mike Tyson's Punch-Out!!, Super Mario Bros., last year's "The History of Minus World Speedruns" (December 2024) or "The History of 120 Star's Early Era" (April 2025) — and tells the history of its record the way a war historian tells a campaign: era by era, runner by runner, heartbreak by heartbreak, over the synth swell of HOME's "We're Finally Landing," an intro so ritualised the first chord now functions as a starting pistol. What has changed is scale. The early videos ran twenty minutes; the recent work runs feature-length, closer to two hours, each one effectively a documentary film researched from forum archives, dead YouTube accounts and Twitch VODs that this channel is often the only institution bothering to preserve. He is a sports historian whose sport happens to be Nintendo.

Speedrunning never had an esports winter, and Summoning Salt is the reason you understand why: it was never a business. It was a priesthood, and he is its chronicler.

And then there is the thing that separates the chronicler from the commentator: he plays. In February 2025, after five years and by his own count roughly seventy-five thousand attempts, he broke the two-minute barrier on Punch-Out!!'s final fight — a record he had spent years narrating other people's failures to reach. Jomboy Media — Jimmy O'Brien — ~2.3M subs

Baseball breakdowns / lip-reading · relentless daily output · NEW ★ Top 50 #50

In November 2019, within hours of The Athletic's report on the Houston Astros, a Yankees fan with editing software posted a video that did what the sport's entire credentialed press corps had not: it showed you the cheating. Bang. Bang. Jimmy O'Brien synced the dugout trash-can audio to the pitches, on screen, checkably, and the sign-stealing scandal stopped being an allegation and became a thing you had heard with your own ears. ESPN later credited the internet with helping crack the case; the internet, in this instance, was one guy. That video is the channel's founding myth and also its thesis: modern sport generates more evidence than its institutions are willing to look at, and a stranger with patience and a timeline can be the most trusted analyst in the room.

The signature format is the breakdown — and the lip-read is the invention. An umpire ejects a manager; the broadcast gives you thirty seconds of distant shouting; Jomboy gives you the screenplay, reconstructed word by word from the mouths, annotated, paced like a two-hander. It is equal parts forensics and comedy, and it created a genre now imitated across every sport. Around it, O'Brien and co-founder Jake Storiale have built an actual media company since 2017 — Talkin' Baseball, Talkin' Yanks, The Warehouse Games, a bench of shows deep enough that the Consistency 96 is simply a description of the machine. And in 2025 came the plot twist that makes this a Competition Issue story: Major League Baseball itself took an equity stake in the company, a negotiation O'Brien says ran four years. The league that got caught by a breakdown video decided the correct long-term response was to own a piece of the desk.

Jomboy proved the scoreboard was lying before the league did. The league's answer, six years later, was to buy shares in the guy who checks.

The review has to hold two things honestly. The work is superb: the Astros file, the ump-show taxonomy, a decade of receipts — and a fan-first warmth that keeps the forensics from curdling into gotcha content. But the record isn't spotless, and we grade the record: the "buzzer" theory the channel amplified in the scandal's aftermath was never substantiated, and this summer a Dodgers prospect pushed back publicly on being breakdown fodder — a fair glimpse of the format's unpriced cost, which is that the people in the clips never volunteered. And the MLB stake cuts both ways forever now. Independence was the product; the shareholder register says otherwise; every future controversy will be read through it. Streaming / invented competition · Spanish-language · EXCELLENT · 81 · does not enter

The esports winter froze every league that tried to become television. Ibai never tried — he became a carnival instead, and carnivals don't have winters.

Dude Perfect — ~62M subs

Trick shots / family sports entertainment · MEDIOCRE · 58 · the negative review, on schedule

Let's establish what this review is not, because sixty-two million subscribers include a lot of eight-year-olds we have no interest in wounding. Dude Perfect is not a scam, not slop, not cynical. Five Texas A&M roommates turned backyard basketball wagers into the biggest sports channel on the platform through fifteen years of genuine skill, genuine friendship, and a family-safe register that is rarer and harder than the internet admits. The trick shots are real; the work ethic is real; the 30 for 30 documentary ESPN gave them in 2024 was earned. When we score this channel 58, we are not saying the men failed. We are saying the format did — and that its failure is the single most instructive one in this entire issue.

Here is the case, and it fits in one sentence: Dude Perfect built an empire on competition with the losing removed. The trick shot is an edit — four hundred attempts, one make, and unlike the twelve-year-old in our Pelé interview, the four hundred stay on the cutting-room floor, because the brand is effortlessness. The "battles" are competitions with nothing at stake, no standings, no consequence, a rotating Rage Monster sketch where even the anger is scheduled. Watch a decade of it back to back — we did — and the pattern becomes unmissable: every element that makes competition mean something (an opponent who wants you to fail, a cost to losing, an outcome you can't reshoot) has been surgically excised, because meaning is risk and risk is off-brand. What remains is astonishingly polished, completely pleasant, and hollow in a way you can hear if you knock on it. Babe Ruth, three tabs over, says it better than we can: no pitcher in the frame, no brag — just talking.

Dude Perfect discovered you could sell competition with the losing removed. Sixty-two million people bought it, which is the most important fact in this issue — because what they bought isn't competition. It's the smile competition makes.

The corporate chapter turned the format's quiet flaw into its explicit strategy. Since October 2024 the group has taken a nine-figure investment from Highmount Capital, hired its first CEO — Andrew Yaffe, out of the NBA's content operation — and pressed on with plans for a $100M-plus theme park outside Dallas, complete with a 330-foot "Impossible Shot" tower. This is a channel becoming a diversified family-entertainment conglomerate, and it explains the scores: main-channel output has thinned toward event-scale Overtime episodes while the machine builds toys, parks, and platforms. Consistency 74 reflects a schedule that now serves a portfolio, not an audience. And their own documentary, to its credit, shows the cost of the smile: the 2012 equity fight, a member saying on camera, "I remember feeling undervalued." The one genuinely high-stakes competition in Dude Perfect history — five friends versus a cap table — is the one they never filmed.

Replay Value 45, because a resolved trick has no tension the second time; X-Factor 50, because the personality long ago merged into the brand and brands do not have X-Factor, they have guidelines. Boss Fight

⚔ BOSS FIGHT ⚔
GOTHAMCHESS vs AGADMATOR · CATEGORY: CHESS CONTENT

Before October 2020, chess YouTube had a king, and it was a soft-spoken Croatian named Antonio Radić who filmed a chessboard, a captured-pieces tray, and nothing else. agadmator was the first chess channel to a million subscribers, the biggest in the world from 2018 onward, built one daily historical game at a time. Then Netflix aired The Queen's Gambit, a pandemic-bored planet discovered that chess.com existed — a million new accounts a month, at the peak — and the boom needed something the archive could not supply: a broadcaster. It found Levy Rozman, an International Master from New York who could make a tournament recap feel like a heavyweight presser, and by late 2021 GothamChess had taken the crown. Today Levy sits near eight million subscribers; agadmator holds around 1.35 million, roughly where the boom left him. The market has spoken. The market, however, does not get to write this magazine, which is why the fight is worth staging.

Because this is the cleanest possible test of a question the whole Competition Issue circles: when a game becomes a spectator sport, what actually gets rewarded — the game, or the coverage of the game? agadmator is the archivist: Morphy, Tal, Fischer, the immortal games, narrated with the same layout and the same gentle cadence since 2016, a museum that happens to upload daily. GothamChess is the broadcaster: recaps, drama, Guess the Elo, the Carlsen–Niemann scandal called like a title fight days after it broke. One of them kept the flame. One of them caught the fire. Only one wins.

Content Quality

▌ SPECIAL FEATURE ▌

The Esports Winter

How the franchised-league dream froze to death, who ate its lunch, and why the couch outlived the stadium. Every number below is documented; the obituary is ours.

For about seven years, the smartest money in entertainment believed competitive gaming was about to become the next NBA, and it priced that belief in public. City-branded teams. Franchise slots selling for eight figures — Evil Geniuses paid a reported $33 million to buy into the LCS in 2020. A $725 million SPAC valuation for FaZe Clan. Overwatch League teams committing tens of millions for the right to exist. The theory was coherent, even elegant: esports had young audiences legacy sport would kill for, so build the legacy-sport container around them — leagues, seasons, media rights, a desk show — and wait for the television money to arrive. The television money did not arrive. And between 2023 and 2025, the container collapsed with a speed that traditional sport, whose failures take generations, has never experienced in its history.

The Body Count

Run the ledger, because the ledger is the story. The Overwatch League — Activision Blizzard's flagship experiment in city franchising — ended after its 2023 season, with roughly two-thirds of its twenty teams voting to take a $6 million exit payment rather than continue; the publisher paid out on the order of $120 million to wind down the thing it had spent years selling as the future. The LCS, North America's League of Legends franchise league, shrank from ten teams to eight in 2024. TSM — one of the region's founding dynasties — had already sold its slot to Shopify Rebellion for around $10 million, roughly a third of what Evil Geniuses had paid for theirs three years earlier; EG itself walked away the following season. The 2023 players' walkout over cut Challengers rosters cost dozens of jobs and delayed a split. FaZe Clan, the SPAC era's poster child, rode its stock from $24.69 to under a dollar inside five months, got its delisting warning, and was eventually acquired for a reported fourteen-to-seventeen million dollars — call it a ninety-seven percent discount on the dream. And in 2024 Riot tried one more reorganisation, merging the LCS with Brazil's and Latin America's leagues into a pan-American "LTA" — which it then dissolved after exactly one season, reviving the leagues it had just merged. The org chart reorganised faster than the audience could learn the acronyms. That is not a downturn. That is a business model politely admitting it never existed.

The esports winter was not a crash in demand for competition. It was a foreclosure on the belief that competition needed to be television first. The viewers never left. They left the format.

What Never Died

Here is the part the obituaries kept missing: through every year of that collapse, the underlying numbers — people playing, people watching people play — stayed enormous. The winter killed a corporate container, not an appetite. You can prove it with a control group, and the control group is this magazine's favourite kind of institution: speedrunning. Games Done Quick has run its charity marathons since 2010 — a couch, a timer, a donation counter — and in the exact years the franchise leagues were liquidating, it just kept metronoming along: roughly two and a half million dollars raised per event, event after event, through 2025 and into 2026. No slots, no city brands, no venture round, and therefore nothing to collapse. Speedrunning never had a winter for the same reason your local pub quiz never had one: it was never financialised enough to freeze. The couch outlived the stadium, and the couch wasn't even trying.

The Ones Who Ate the Lunch

Meanwhile, the audience the leagues were built to capture showed up somewhere else entirely — at competitions run by the entertainers themselves. The case study is Ibai Llanos, reviewed in full this issue: La Velada del Año, an amateur boxing card for streamers, grew in five editions from a Barcelona club night into the single most-watched individual livestream in the history of the internet — a peak above nine million concurrent viewers in 2025, with the 2026 edition simulcast across three platforms. The Kings League, Piqué and Ibai's game-designed seven-a-side football, drew nation-scale audiences to a competition that was born as content. The Sidemen sold out Wembley — ninety thousand seats — for a charity football match that peaked at 2.7 million concurrent viewers and raised £4.7 million. Note what these events did not have: franchises, media-rights decks, a league office. Note what they did have: creators the audience already loved, stakes the audience could feel, and a broadcast that belonged to the people in it. The franchised leagues spent a decade trying to bolt an audience onto a structure. The creators bolted a structure onto an audience. Only one of those directions works, and everyone now knows which. Honesty requires the counterexample, so here it is: Creator Clash 2 halved its predecessor's buys, paid none of its pledged charity money, and lost a quarter of a million dollars. Creator-run is a direction, not a guarantee — the events that thrive are the ones where the community was real before the ring was.

The Complicating Space Heater

And now the fact that ruins the tidy narrative, which is why it gets its own section. While the Western league model froze, the biggest prize pools in esports history were being written — in Riyadh. The Esports World Cup paid out $62.5 million in 2024 and $71.5 million in 2025, bankrolled by a Saudi sovereign-wealth apparatus that has put a reported $38 billion into gaming through Savvy Games Group — which, for good measure, also owns the operator now running Overwatch's replacement circuit. So the honest map of post-winter esports has two continents: a creator-carnival economy that runs on communities, and a sovereign-spectacle economy that runs on strategic budgets and does not especially need an audience economics to close. Between them, the old middle — the venture-funded, city-franchised, television-shaped league — is simply gone. What that second continent buys, and what it costs a competitive scene to be someone's national strategy, is a question esports has mostly declined to ask out loud, and this magazine notes — for the record — that the silence is itself an answer of sorts.

What the Winter Teaches

Strip it to the mechanism and the lesson is the same one running through every section of this issue. Competition is not a format; it is a relationship — somebody sets a challenge, somebody swears they can beat it, and a crowd gathers that cares about the somebodies. The franchise era failed because it tried to sell the crowd a structure before it had given them anyone to care about; it built the stadium and assumed the belonging would follow the seating chart. Everything that survived the winter — the speedrun marathon, the streamer boxing night, the chess boom the Boss Fight tab is arguing about right now — got the order right: community first, competition grown out of it, structure last and lightly. This magazine, a scoreboard-shaped publication on a scoreboard-shaped platform, would only add: the audience was never confused. Nine million people did not misunderstand what they wanted when they watched a streamer's boxing night instead of a franchise final. They wanted stakes carried by people they knew. They always did. The only ones who misunderstood were the ones holding the spreadsheets — and the spreadsheet, this issue keeps finding, is where competition goes to die. ▌ DEATH OF FORMAT ▌

Game Over

Five ways competition got turned into retention bait. The format is the corpse — never the audience. Autopsies below.

☠
The "[Team] Is In SHAMBLES" Rage Merchant

Every fanbase now has one: the daily apocalypse channel, thumbnail face mid-scream, announcing that the club is FINISHED, the manager is a FRAUD, and the transfer window was a BETRAYAL — twenty-four hours after announcing the identical collapse from the opposite direction. It exists because sports fandom is the most reliable outrage reservoir ever discovered: the games arrive on a schedule, half the audience is guaranteed to be furious after each one, and the algorithm cannot tell the difference between analysis and a man burning his own jersey for the fourth consecutive Tuesday. The tragedy is what it displaces. Actual fan analysis — tactics, context, the long view — exists on the platform and is routinely out-recommended by a grown adult screaming at a badge. The rage merchant doesn't cover the competition. He is one: a daily contest to be angriest first, and the standings reset at midnight.

He has declared the season over forty times this season. A man who is always furious is providing weather, not analysis — and the club he's screaming about will outlive his larynx.
☠
The AI-Voiced "TRAGIC Story of…" Sports Documentary

The thumbnail is a star athlete in grayscale. The title promises the UNTOLD, TRAGIC, or HEARTBREAKING story. The video is a Wikipedia career summary read by a synthetic baritone over licensed highlight scraps and slow zooms on press photos, at a volume of dozens per week per channel, for every athlete with a name worth searching. This is the slop wave the Machine Issue mapped, arrived at the stadium: the past of sport is infinite, public-domain-adjacent, and emotionally pre-loaded, which makes it perfect strip-mining territory — no expert to consult, no fan who was there to object, no claim precise enough to be falsified. The tell is the grief without the knowledge: the "tragic story" that cannot name the injury, the "untold story" told identically by nine channels the same week. Real sports documentary — the kind Jomboy or a 30 for 30 does with receipts — requires knowing something. This requires only that you once existed and can be searched.

A career is not a content mine, and grief is not a genre setting. If the narrator can't pronounce the club's name, the story was never going to be told — only extracted.
☠
The Fabricated Trick-Shot Reel

Downstream of the trick-shot empires came the counterfeiters: channels whose impossible shots are not improbable-but-earned, they are edited — reversed footage, hidden cuts, compositing, physics that would make a ping-pong ball file a complaint. The format thrives because the feed rewards the reaction, not the verification: a fake make and a real make produce the same three seconds of dopamine, and only one of them costs four hundred attempts. This is the trend that makes our Dude Perfect review look almost heroic by contrast — they, at least, genuinely make the shots they show you. But the counterfeiters are also the format's logical conclusion, and that's the autopsy finding worth keeping: once a genre establishes that the attempts don't matter and only the make gets shown, it has already taught its audience that the process is disposable. Faking the make is just the next efficiency. A competition where you can't see the trying was always one edit away from a competition where nobody tried at all.

The fake trick shot didn't corrupt the genre — it graduated from it. Once you've sold competition with the failure removed, removing the success's reality is barely a step.
☠
The Betting-App "Analysis" Channel

It walks like analysis. It talks like analysis — form guides, matchup stats, a confident man with a screen. And every road in it leads to a promo code. The betting-pick channel is the most cynical format on this page because it borrows the entire costume of sports expertise to do the opposite of expertise's job: the analysis exists to generate the pick, the pick exists to generate the deposit, and the channel's actual customer is not the viewer but the sportsbook whose sign-up bonus lives in the description. The record of past picks — the one piece of accountability that would make the format honest — is curated, deleted, or simply never mentioned; wins are pinned, losses evaporate; the highlight reel logic of the trick-shot channel applied to your rent money. Sport spent a century building an audience's trust in the person with the statistics. This format spends that trust at the window and keeps the vig.

An analyst who profits when you lose is not an analyst — he's a tout with a thumbnail. Show the full pick history or admit the description link is the entire show.
☠
The "Ranking Every…" Tier-List Mill

Ranking EVERY champion. EVERY stadium. EVERY season, kit, mascot, goal celebration. The tier-list mill is what happens when the shape of competition gets harvested and the substance composted: a format that produces the feeling of standings — S through F, the little draggable icons, the pleasant finality of placement — without a single argued criterion underneath. It exists because ranking is the cheapest possible content to generate (opinions are free, grids are free, the catalogue is pre-made) and the most reliable to engage, since every placement is a tiny outrage machine addressed personally to someone's childhood. We are aware, thank you, of the glass house this magazine ranks from. The difference is the working: a ranking is criticism only if each placement is an argument that could be wrong — with reasons stated, evidence named, and the record kept. Strip the arguments and a tier list is just fifty small provocations in a trench coat, and the mill strips the arguments first, because arguments take time and the grid doesn't.

A ranking without reasons is a Ouija board with extra steps. Yes, we publish a Top 50. Ours prints its working — that's not a defence, it's the entire genre's price of admission.
