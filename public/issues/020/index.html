▌ EDITOR'S LETTER ▌

The Form That Grew Here

The essay is the one literary form YouTube invented for itself. Everything else on the platform arrived with luggage: the sketch came from television, the lecture from the classroom, the vlog from the diary, the review from magazines like the one we are, frankly, dressed up as. The video essay is the exception. It is the platform's native literature — the one thing it grew rather than imported — and twenty issues in, we have finally done the obvious thing and graded it like one.

Precision first, because this issue is about a form that lives or dies on it. YouTube did not invent the idea of an essay made of moving pictures. Film scholars and critics had been cutting them for years, mostly for festivals and seminar rooms and an audience you could seat in a lecture theatre. What the platform did was turn a specialist exercise into a mass form, with an audience, an economy, a star system, and a canon. And if the form has a birthday, ours is April 2014, when Tony Zhou and Taylor Ramos uploaded the first Every Frame a Painting essay — on Bong Joon-ho's Mother — and proved something criticism had never been able to prove at scale: that an argument about a film could be made out of the film. Prose describes a cut. The video essay shows you the cut, slows it down, puts it next to another cut, and lets the evidence and the argument share a frame. Every critic before them was working from memory and a thesaurus. Zhou and Ramos were working from the footage. Twenty-eight essays later they stopped, and they still hold #3 in our Top 50 — a seat awarded on the archive, because the archive does not decay.

What grew in that room after they left is the subject of this issue. The form now has monarchs — essayists who hold our highest verdict and fill a weekend with a single upload. It has an economy, mostly paid in monthly instalments by strangers, and its own streaming service, owned by the people who make it. It has a plagiarism reckoning behind it, the December 2023 audit that proved the form was finally serious enough to be worth stealing from. And it has a canon. Canons exist to be argued with. So we argue.

A word about the number on the cover, and then never again. This is our twentieth issue. No cake. No retrospective, no montage of our own covers, no letter from the editor congratulating the editor. A magazine that has spent nineteen issues writing essays about YouTube has, at last, written one about the YouTube essay, and the only proportionate response is mild embarrassment that it took this long.

The lineup earns the theme. The Boss Fight is the first in this magazine's history in which both fighters hold ESSENTIAL: Jacob Geller, the lyrical connector, against Jenny Nicholson, the comic litigator, 91 against 91, and somebody still has to lose. Player Profiles brings an 89 to the door of ESSENTIAL — hbomberguy, entering the Top 50 at #17 and kept on the wrong side of the 90 line by the calendar alone. Folding Ideas and F.D Signifier both score 84, both EXCELLENT, and both stay outside, which swells the 84-tier waiting room to nine channels: the fullest it has been in the ranking's history, and a problem we print rather than hide. TED-Ed leaves the table to make room. Al-Daheeh becomes our first Arabic Player Profile. Time Capsule summons five dead essayists — Montaigne, who named the form; Woolf, Orwell, Sei Shōnagon, E.B. White — and shows them what their descendants do with a microphone and a timeline. Cheat Codes follows the money into the spare bedroom. Game Over performs five autopsies on the ways the form gets faked. Hidden Levels finds five real essayists you can subscribe to tonight. And Yob, as is now traditional, is waiting by the letterbox.

One last thing, because the word itself is the best argument for the form. Montaigne's essais were attempts — the French verb means to try. An essay was never supposed to be a verdict; it was a mind testing itself against a subject in public, with the working left visible. Which is exactly what the best of YouTube's essayists do, and exactly what this magazine tries to do with every score it prints. We are attempting, too. Read the attempts. Argue with them.

Every other format on YouTube was imported. The essay grew here — the first literature ever written in footage, by people who could finally quote the thing they were arguing about.

— The Editor

▶ THE COMPLAINTS DEPARTMENT
hbomberguy Said "Adobe Must Die First." By March, Adobe Animate Had Stopped Updating

On 11 August 2025, Harry Brewis — hbomberguy to the internet — released "Adobe Must Die First," a roughly thirty-five-minute essay whose title is also its thesis and its tone. In March 2026, Adobe ended updates to Adobe Animate, the program that grew out of Flash, the software an entire generation of web animators learned inside — a generation Brewis had already written the eulogy for in 2024's "How Newgrounds Ruined My Life." We claim no causation. Software companies do not retire products because a man from Halifax made a video about them, and nobody should pretend otherwise. But read in sequence, the two events describe a job the video essay has quietly taken on: the platform's complaints department, with citations. The researched, angry, named-and-shamed long-form piece used to be consumer journalism's work. On YouTube it is increasingly the essayist's. The plagiarism video was the form auditing itself; this is the form auditing everyone else. hbomberguy's profile is inside, and he enters the Top 50 at #17 on an 89 — held out of ESSENTIAL by the one axis no amount of fury can fix, which is the calendar.

▶ THE FOUNDERS RETURN
Every Frame a Painting Came Back — to a Genre That Grew Monarchs While It Was Away

Twenty-eight essays between April 2014 and September 2016; then, in December 2017, a public post-mortem from Tony Zhou and Taylor Ramos declaring the channel finished, on the rule that they would stop when it stopped being fun. In August 2024 they came back: new work, the first since 2016, arriving alongside a short film. Sit with what they came back to. When they left, the video essay was a promising format with one obvious model — theirs: eight-to-fifteen minutes, one argument, every clip doing a job. When they returned, the form had essayists holding our highest verdict, its own creator-owned streaming service, a plagiarism scandal in its recent past, and four-hour runtimes treated as a normal evening. Some of that is their legacy and some of it is a direction they never pointed. Our position is unchanged, and pleasingly so: Every Frame a Painting holds #3 in the Top 50 at 92, a seat awarded on the archive while the channel was silent. The return does not move the score, because the score was never waiting for it. The archive was always enough. The new work is a bonus round.

▶ THE GUILD BECOMES A STUDIO
Nebula at Seven: The Essayists' Streaming Service Has Started Commissioning

Nebula launched on 23 May 2019 with 75 creators, built by Dave Wiskus's Standard Broadcast as a creator-owned complement to YouTube — not a replacement, a second room. The reported structure was the radical part: profits split 50/50 between Standard and its creators, divided by watch time. Then the milestones stacked up. A reported 100,000 monthly paying subscribers by July 2020, with later reports putting the figure well into the hundreds of thousands (the numbers vary by source, and we print the hedge). A minority stake from Curiosity Stream in September 2021, at a valuation reported above $50 million. Sam Denby — of Wendover Productions — named chief content officer in August 2023. And in 2025, the announcement of Nebula's first scripted original, Sub/liminal. Trace the arc and a guild has turned into a studio: a cooperative of essayists that now commissions work rather than merely hosting it. F.D Signifier, profiled this issue, is a Nebula member channel. Our Cheat Codes feature argues Nebula is the only room in the essay economy its tenants actually own — mostly. A minority stake is still somebody else's key.

▶ THE ESSAY AS EVENT
F.D Signifier's Four-Hour Tyler Perry Essay Got Written Up Like a Premiere

On 14 February 2026, F.D Signifier released "The Tyler Perry Video": roughly four hours arguing that Perry's output is creatively bankrupt, built on recurring colorism and corrosive religious and gender tropes. Aftermath covered it under the headline "The Bell Finally Tolls for Tyler Perry" — an outlet writing up a YouTube essay the way an arts desk writes up a film opening. That is the shift worth logging. The long essay is no longer something you stumble into at midnight via autoplay; it is a release, announced and anticipated, that people clear an evening for and argue about afterwards in print. Four hours is not a video; it is a double feature with a thesis. Which is the strength and the problem at once, and our profile of F.D Signifier this issue takes both seriously: a sociological spine, an academic's patience, a barbershop register — and a runtime that works as a moat and a wall in equal measure. He scores 84, EXCELLENT, and does not enter the Top 50. The essay is an event now. Events, by definition, do not happen every week.

▶ THE ECONOMICS, MADE VISIBLE
Folding Ideas' "Belle and the Meaning of Place": Patrons in March, Everyone Else in July

Dan Olson's "Belle and the Meaning of Place" went to Folding Ideas' patrons first, in March 2026, and reached the public YouTube channel on 7 July 2026. As far as the public channel shows — and as far as we could verify — it was the first new mainline essay there since "This Is Financial Advice" in September 2023: a public gap of roughly two and a half years. The patrons were not in the dark during that stretch, which is precisely the point. The patron-first window is the essay economy printed on a calendar: the people who pay for the room get the first look at what was made in it. Nothing about that is scandalous, and much about it is fair. But it quietly changes what a public channel is. It becomes a shop window for work paid for somewhere else — and a shop window, however beautiful, is a terrible thing to subscribe to. Our review of Folding Ideas grades exactly that consequence: some of the best essays the form has produced, an 84, a Consistency score of 38, and no seat in the Top 50. The OverSimplified precedent holds. Quality cannot outvote a calendar.

Time Capsule

Five writers who made a literature out of trying, shown the form that borrowed their word. This issue opens the ESSAYISTS shelf: the Gascon nobleman who named the essay after the act of attempting, the court lady whose private notebook got loose a thousand years before everybody else's, the pamphleteer who wrote the rules for honest English, the Maine farmer who learned "omit needless words" from the man who first shouted it — and Virginia Woolf. She is the last figure drawn from the magazine's original legacy pool. With her interview printed, the reserve is four musicians, full stop. Every one of them watches our YouTube from inside their own century, and the gap between the two is where the criticism happens.

⚠ SATIRICAL / FICTIONAL — Michel de Montaigne did not participate in this Q&A.
Michel de Montaigne
1533–1592 · Seigneur de Montaigne, author of the Essais · responding from his tower library near Bordeaux, c. 1586, between editions
C+W
Monsieur de Montaigne, you named a form. You called your book Essais — attempts, trials. We'd like to show you what the word became. Millions of people now make what they call "video essays."
MONTAIGNE
[he is at the window of the round room, looking down at the vines, and does not turn until he hears the word "millions"] Millions of attempts? [turning, delighted] Then I have finally done something useful. When I began, my neighbours assumed a gentleman who wrote about himself was either ill or idle, and in my case they were right on both counts. [lowers himself into the chair with exaggerated care — the stone has been troubling him again this month] Show me one. No — show me the little pictures first, the whole wall of them. A man should see the doors before he chooses a house.
C+W
That's the thumbnail wall. Every video advertises itself with one picture and a few words.
MONTAIGNE
[reading aloud, slowly, with the patience of a man who was taught Latin before French] "The TRUTH about." "Why everyone is WRONG about." "The REAL reason." And the faces — every one with its mouth open, as though it had just been told the price of wheat. [a dry laugh] Ten years ago I had a medal struck. A pair of scales, perfectly level, and around them: Que sais-je? What do I know? Up there on the beam [pointing at the painted rafters] I had them letter "I suspend judgment," in Greek, so I would be obliged to read it every time I lost my temper with a book. Your young people have struck the opposite medal ten thousand times. Each little picture says: I know, you do not, and it will take me forty minutes to tell you. [peering closer] Forty minutes to explain something they were already certain of before they began. I have never managed to be certain of anything for forty minutes together.
C+W
Watch one, then. This runs an hour, on a single old film. It wanders — a childhood memory, a detour through a painting, a tangent about a dead poet — and finds its way back.
MONTAIGNE
[watches for a long while, and twice laughs out loud at something the interviewer cannot see] This boy writes the way I walk. Up and down this room, a new thought at every turn of the wall, and not one of them the thought I climbed the stairs to have. My ideas follow one another, I wrote, but sometimes at a distance. [jabbing a finger at the screen] He went into the painting and I thought, he is lost. And he was lost — and it was the best part. The old Greek who wandered off to Egypt in the middle of his history wandered for the sake of the story. This one wanders for the sake of the wandering. That is a much rarer vice, and I should know, since it is my only talent.
C+W
The makers are shown a graph of exactly where their audience leaves, second by second. The system favours the videos people don't leave. Most of them learn to cut the detour.
MONTAIGNE
[the delight goes out of him slowly, the way a fire is banked for the night] So the reader's boredom is measured, and shown to the writer, and the writer is paid by it. [quietly] Do you know how many readers I have lost? I have no idea. That ignorance is my entire liberty. I warned them on the first page: I am myself the matter of my book; you would be unreasonable to spend your leisure on so frivolous a subject; so farewell. I told them to go away! The ones who stayed, stayed for the wandering. [tapping the arm of the chair, once per word] Had anyone shown me the exact page where each reader shut the book, I would have cut that page. And it would have been the chapter on thumbs, or the one about falling off my horse — the page some single reader four hundred years from now loves more than the rest of me put together. Your graph is a perfect portrait of the average reader. I never once wrote for him.
C+W
One of the series that founded the form — Every Frame a Painting — made short film essays for a couple of years and then stopped. They said their rule was to stop when it stopped being fun. Years later, they came back.
MONTAIGNE
[sits up so sharply that the stone makes him wince, and he does not care in the least] They stopped when it stopped being pleasant? [beaming] I came up to this tower at thirty-eight for precisely that reason. I had it painted on the wall of the study next door — I am not exaggerating, go and look — that I was weary of the servitude of the court and of public employments, and meant to spend what remained in freedom and leisure. And then they came back? Good. So did I; they made me mayor of Bordeaux against my wishes, and I went, because one must. And every year I add to my book. I add, but I do not correct. [immensely pleased with himself] If those two came back and added, without disowning what they made when they were younger, then they understand the form better than most of the people who never stopped.
C+W
Most of them can't stop when it stops being fun. The essay is how they pay the rent.
MONTAIGNE
[opens his mouth to answer at once — and then, which is rare for him, closes it again] Ah. [a long look around the round room: the five curved shelves, the window over his own vineyards] You are about to tell me that a man whose father had him woken every morning of his childhood by a musician, so as not to startle his tender brain, is in no position to lecture anyone about leisure. [a rueful laugh] You are right. I wrote that a man must keep a back-shop all his own, a little room behind the shop where he is entirely free and no one else comes. I meant it for everybody. I was picturing a man who owned the shop. [more quietly] Your young people have no back-shop. They have let it to the customers. They sit in it with the door open while the customers watch them think, and they are paid by how long the customers stay. I do not know what my book would have become in that room. Shorter, I suspect. And more certain. I should like to believe I would have refused. I had a vineyard. I have no idea.
C+W
Some of them have dropped the films and the paintings entirely. The subject is just themselves. Their morning, their flat, their cat.
MONTAIGNE
Show me the cat. [watches a woman narrate her morning while a cat walks deliberately across her writing-desk and sits on the work] When I play with my cat, who knows whether I am her pastime more than she is mine? [nodding at the screen] She is directing this film. The woman has simply not been told. [then, more seriously] I am the last man alive entitled to sneer at this. I have written about my kidney stones, my habits at table, my wretched memory. But mark one difference. I studied myself because I was the only specimen I could watch honestly — I was the instrument, never the point. When this woman says "my morning," is she looking at herself, or at herself being looked at? The first is an essay. The second is a mirror, and a mirror only ever told me what I was already afraid of.
C+W
The most successful essays now end with a verdict. A thesis in the first minute, proven by the last. "And that is why this film is a masterpiece."
MONTAIGNE
[genuine alarm, as if someone had carried a lantern too close to the books] Proven? [stands, despite everything, and goes back to the window] Understand what you are telling me. I gave the form its name because I could not finish anything. Every chapter of mine ends with me less sure than when it began, and that is how I know it was honest. An essay that knows its last sentence before it writes its first is a sermon wearing my hat. [turning back, the humour returning, because with him it always does] Still — millions, you said. Millions of attempts. Some of them must end badly, unresolved, face down in a ditch. Find me those. Those are mine.
C+W
Last question. If you made one — what would your little picture say?
MONTAIGNE
[considers the wall of open mouths for a long moment, and then his own face, faint in the dark glass beside them] My face, I suppose, since I am the subject. Not with the mouth open. With the expression I wear when I reach the bottom of a page and discover I have been arguing for the other side. [smiling] And the words —
"Que sais-je? — with a question mark where your young people put their arrow. Tell them the word they borrowed means attempt, not answer. The day one of them finishes an essay certain, he has not written one. He has only stopped walking."
⚠ SATIRICAL / FICTIONAL — Virginia Woolf did not participate in this Q&A.
Virginia Woolf
1882–1941 · novelist, essayist, printer · responding from Tavistock Square, Bloomsbury, autumn 1929, just after A Room of One's Own · the last name on the magazine's oldest list
C+W
Mrs Woolf, we'd like to show you a woman making an essay. It runs an hour. She made it alone, in her spare bedroom.
VIRGINIA WOOLF
[she watches without speaking for some minutes, a cigarette burning down forgotten between two fingers] She has a room. [leaning in] She has hung quilts on the walls. Why the quilts? [told: to deaden the echo] To keep the world's noise out, or her own voice in. Both, I expect. [a small, pleased laugh] Last October I told two halls of young women at Cambridge that they must have money and a room of their own if they were to write, and they took it down very politely, and I could see on their faces that they thought it a counsel of perfection. And look at her: a room, a lamp, a door that shuts, and the entire world on the far side of a little glass eye, listening. [more quietly] On or about December 1910, I once said, human character changed. You have the air of people about to tell me it changed again.
C+W
It did. Money first, then. Most of these essayists can't live on what the platform pays. They're kept by a page — Patreon, it's called — where strangers pledge a few shillings a month.
VIRGINIA WOOLF
[reading the tiers aloud, with a novelist's ear for exactly how absurd they are] "Supporter." "Patron." "Executive Producer — your name in the credits." [setting the cigarette down at last] Patrons. Dr Johnson wrote the finest letter in the language to be rid of one — a lord who watched with unconcern while a man struggled for life in the water and then, once he had reached the shore, encumbered him with help. [considering] But this is not a lord. These are — thousands? A thousand small patrons is a different animal from one large one; no single one of them can tell her what to write. My own five hundred a year, in the book, came from an aunt who fell off her horse in Bombay. Very obliging of her. These women have no aunt. They have the public, by instalments. I cannot decide whether that is the most democratic aunt ever invented or only the most exacting.
C+W
The form has no fixed shape. An essay here can be about a theme park, a dead video game, a plagiarist, the colour of the light in a film — sometimes all four at once.
VIRGINIA WOOLF
[delighted, and not troubling to hide it] Of course it can. The essay is the one form with no walls. A novel must at least pretend to a plot, poor thing; the essay may pick up a moth on a windowpane and set it down an hour later as the meaning of death, and nobody may object, provided we were under the spell the whole way. I wrote that the essay must lap us about and draw its curtain across the world. [gesturing at the screen] Look what she has done with the curtain. She has drawn it across a bedroom and opened it onto everyone. [studying the woman's face as she talks] And the voice. In print one has to manufacture a voice out of commas. She simply has one. Her pauses do the work of my semicolons — and rather better, I am sorry to say.
C+W
You'll want to know about the women. Some of the form's most celebrated essayists are women — two of them sit in the top twenty of our own rankings, one with essays that run for hours. Nobody stood at the door to let them in.
VIRGINIA WOOLF
[instantly, the whole face alight] Nobody at the door! In my little book a beadle waves me off the turf, because only Fellows and Scholars may walk on the grass, and a gentleman at the library informs me that ladies are admitted only if accompanied by a Fellow or furnished with a letter of introduction. And she simply — began? [then the light dims, by a precise degree] You said nobody stood at the door. You did not say nobody stood in the garden. [glancing at the long column of comments beneath the video, then away, as one glances away from an accident] I have read reviews of women's books. I can imagine what the strangers write underneath. It will be what the learned professor wrote in his great volume on the mental, moral and physical inferiority of the female sex — only faster, and without the footnotes. The room has a door. I gather it has not got a lock.
C+W
The money is the harder door. An hour-long essay can take months, unpaid until the patrons decide it was worth it. The people who can make them tend to be the ones who can afford to wait.
VIRGINIA WOOLF
[quiet for a long moment; when she answers it is with great precision] Then you have built a great many rooms and kept the old rent. [she rises and goes to the window over the square] I gave Shakespeare a sister, Judith, every bit as gifted as he, who could not get a room or a stage or an hour to herself, and so died with all of it unwritten. Your little machine, you tell me, costs almost nothing. The room costs nothing; the audience costs nothing. And the months cost exactly what they always cost. [turning back] Find me the girl who shares a bedroom with two sisters and stands behind a shop counter all day and has the essay in her, and cannot afford six unpaid months to let it out. She is Judith. You have given her a microphone. You have not given her an afternoon.
C+W
Some of them have gone further. They've built a service of their own alongside the big one — run with the essayists, splitting the takings with them.
VIRGINIA WOOLF
[turning sharply, and for the first time laughing without any reserve at all] Their own press! Leonard and I bought a little hand press and set it up at home in Richmond — I set the type with my own fingers, slowly and very badly — and it was the wisest thing we ever did, because it meant no editor would ever again stand between me and the page. I am, I told my diary, the only woman in England free to write what I like. [sitting again, fierce now] Tell those young people the room was never the freedom. Every one of them has a room. The freedom is owning the press the room prints on. The moment some gentleman somewhere owns the machine that decides whether her essay is shown, she is back in the library doorway, waiting for a Fellow.
C+W
Her essay has been watched by millions. Underneath, they write back. "Slay." "This ate." A crying face. A skull.
VIRGINIA WOOLF
[the face changes — a faint, involuntary withdrawal, like a hand from a kettle] A skull means she has done well? [told: yes, approximately] Then she has been dead and praised in a single breath, which will at least prepare her for posterity. [the laugh comes a shade too quickly] I confess I flinch. I have lived my whole life among people who can tell a semicolon from a sneeze, and when I hear "millions" of anything I hear a football crowd. One wanted the common reader. This looks rather more like the common —
C+W
You took "the common reader" from Dr Johnson — the reader uncorrupted by literary prejudice, whose verdict he trusted over the scholars'. You put it on the title page of your essays. Isn't that them?
VIRGINIA WOOLF
[a pause; then, to her very great credit, she laughs at herself properly] Caught. Caught in the doorway like a beadle. [stubbing out the cigarette] Yes. Johnson rejoiced to concur with the common reader, and I borrowed his rejoicing for my title page, and I meant every word — and the moment the common reader arrives in his millions, using a skull for an adjective, I want a Fellow to accompany him. Snobbery is the one luggage one never forgets to pack. Very well; I withdraw the crowd. Somewhere in it, surely, is a girl in a shop with a quilt on her wall, listening to another woman think aloud for an hour, and then going home to try. If one in a million of them does, that is a great many more Judiths than England managed in three hundred years.
C+W
Last thing. This issue's lead feature borrows your title. It's called "A Channel of One's Own."
VIRGINIA WOOLF
[one eyebrow, then the slow smile of a woman who has been borrowed from by better and worse] A channel. Of course — nobody keeps a room any more; one must keep a canal, with traffic on it. [then, serious, looking back at the woman on the screen in her quilted room] Borrow it by all means. Only print the first half of my sentence as well. Everybody remembers the room. Nobody remembers that I put the money first.
"Money and a room of her own, I said. You have built the rooms by the million, every one with a window onto the whole world — and left the money to be passed in through the window, a coin at a time, by whoever happens to be looking. It is a great deal more than she had. But a room one must be watched in to afford is not yet, quite, a room of one's own."
⚠ SATIRICAL / FICTIONAL — George Orwell did not participate in this Q&A.
George Orwell
1903–1950 · essayist, novelist, pamphlet collector · responding from Canonbury Square, London, spring 1946, just after "Politics and the English Language"
C+W
Mr Orwell, you've just published six rules for clear English. We'd like you to read some titles. These are the names people give their political essays now.
GEORGE ORWELL
[he rolls a cigarette from a tin of shag before he looks, licks the paper, and only then reads — aloud, in the thin, flattened voice a Spanish bullet left him] "Student DESTROYS professor." "Commentator OBLITERATED with facts and logic." "Activist gets OWNED." [lights the cigarette, slowly] Nobody was destroyed. I was in Cologne last spring; I know what destroyed looks like. This is a young man in a cardigan losing an argument about rent. [taps the ash] Rule one: never use a metaphor you are used to seeing in print. These are past even that. "Destroyed" has gone the way of "ride roughshod over" and "stand shoulder to shoulder with" — it stopped being a picture years ago, and now it's only a noise meaning "my side won."
C+W
Inside the videos, the same phrases come round again and again. "Let that sink in." "Do your own research." "Wake up." "Think about that for a second."
GEORGE ORWELL
[copying them onto the lid of the tobacco tin in pencil — a man who collects specimens and can't help it] "Let that sink in." [grimly amused] That one is almost beautiful. It's an instruction to stop thinking, dressed up as an invitation to start. The speaker has said something, and instead of proving it he tells it to sink — into you, like a stone, without passing through anything on the way down. I wrote that modern prose consists more and more of phrases tacked together like the sections of a prefabricated hen-house. You've had the whole hen-house delivered, painted, with the hens already in it. The ready-made phrase does a man's thinking for him. What people never notice is that it does his feeling for him too.
C+W
Political argument is one of the biggest genres. Young men take a camera onto university campuses, debate students, and the clips are cut into compilations. The best moments get millions of views.
GEORGE ORWELL

▌ SPECIAL FEATURE ▌

A Channel of One's Own

What the video essay costs, and who pays. Every fact below is documented where we say it is; the argument, and any bad temper, is ours.

In October 1928, Virginia Woolf gave two lectures to the women of Newnham and Girton colleges at Cambridge, and the following year she published them as A Room of One's Own. Its thesis fits on a sticky note and has outlived nearly everything written around it: "a woman must have money and a room of her own if she is to write fiction." People remember the line as a romantic one. Read the book and it is closer to accountancy. Woolf was not arguing that genius needs inspiration; she was arguing that genius needs an income, a door, and time nobody else can claim. "Intellectual freedom depends upon material things," she wrote, and then she named the materials with the precision of a bursar — five hundred a year, and a room with a lock. "Five hundred a year stands for the power to contemplate," she explained, and "a lock on the door means the power to think for oneself."

Ninety-odd years later, the most ambitious writing on YouTube is done by people who solved Woolf's problem in a way she could not have imagined, and the platform they work on never meant to help them do it. This feature is about that solution: what it costs to make a video essay, who actually pays, and why the economics of the form explain its greatest work, its runtime habit, its one great scandal, and its fragility — all at once.

Five Hundred a Year, Paid Monthly

Start with the thing the algorithm cannot do. YouTube's native economy pays per view, and views reward volume: upload often, upload reliably, give the recommendation engine something fresh to serve. Run the video essay through that logic and it makes no sense. A serious essay can take months of research, scripting, sourcing, and editing to produce one upload — and in the ad economy, a month without uploads is a month without income. Measured by the platform's own incentives, the long-form essayist is a business plan that should have died in the spreadsheet.

It survived because of patronage. The essay economy runs heavily on monthly pledges — a few dollars a month from a great many viewers who have decided that this particular mind deserves time. hbomberguy's Patreon, at last check, counted its members in the tens of thousands. That is Woolf's five hundred a year, crowdsourced and paid in instalments. And the crucial feature of a patron's money is the one an advertiser's money lacks: it arrives whether or not you uploaded this month. Patronage does not pay for videos. It pays for time — which is the essay's only real raw material. Map the rest of Woolf's settlement onto the present and it holds almost comically well. The room of one's own is a spare bedroom with blankets on the walls. The lock on the door is a USB microphone and an editing timeline nobody else has the password to. The power to contemplate is a monthly pledge that lets someone disappear for a season and come back with an argument.

Woolf asked for five hundred a year and a lock on the door. The video essayists got it in monthly instalments from strangers — and wrote a literature in the spare room.

The Room Gets Bigger: The Runtime Drift

Look at what the form became once the rooms existed. In the 2014 era, a serious video essay ran about ten minutes; Every Frame a Painting built entire arguments inside that span and made brevity look like a discipline. Around 2020, the hour-plus essay stopped being an exception and became a genre — a shift InsideHook's reporting tied in part to the pandemic. A captive audience, as we would put it, with evenings to give away. Today the event essay runs three or four hours and is announced like a premiere.

The flattering version of that story says patrons bought depth, and depth takes time to deliver. That version is true, and incomplete. The runtime drift has two parents. One is the patron, who pays for a mind to go further than the algorithm would ever commission. The other is the algorithm itself, which pays for watch time and discovered, somewhere around the same years, that it liked long things very much. A four-hour essay is a work of criticism; it is also, unavoidably, the most efficient retention object the platform has ever been handed. The best essayists are long because the argument needs the room. The worst are long because the second parent noticed. Our Game Over section this issue performs the autopsy on that second child — the runtime flex, length worn as a medal — and it is worth saying here that it is a sibling of the good work, not a stranger to it. Same house. Different upbringing.

The Guild That Bought the Building

Rooms rented from a platform can be repossessed by a platform. Some creators saw that early and did something unusual about it. Nebula launched on 23 May 2019 with 75 creators, built by Dave Wiskus's Standard Broadcast as a creator-owned complement to YouTube. The reported deal is the whole point: profits split 50/50 between Standard and the creators, divided according to watch time. Reported subscriber figures have been healthy and variable — 100,000 monthly paying subscribers by July 2020, and later reports well into the hundreds of thousands, depending on who is counting. In September 2021 Curiosity Stream took a minority stake at a valuation reported above $50 million. In August 2023 Sam Denby became chief content officer; in 2025 Nebula announced its first scripted original, Sub/liminal.

Read in Woolf's terms, Nebula is the lock on the door. Every other room in the essay economy is rented — from YouTube, whose terms and taste change without consultation, and from the patronage platforms, whose fees and policies the essayists do not set. Nebula is the one room whose tenants hold a share of the deeds. "Mostly" is the honest qualifier: a minority outside stake is still somebody else's key on the ring. But a cooperative of essayists that now commissions scripted work has moved from tenant to landlord in seven years, and nothing else in the creator economy has made that trip quite so quietly.

The Window Before the Window

Folding Ideas is the clearest demonstration of what the patron model can produce at full power — and what it costs the public. In January 2022, Dan Olson published "Line Goes Up – The Problem With NFTs," a 137-minute essay that nobody commissioned and that drew formal responses from Time, Nasdaq, Decrypt, and Cointelegraph. An industry felt obliged to answer a man in Calgary. No advertiser would have funded the months behind it; no recommendation engine would have asked for it. Patrons did, by the simple act of paying for the time.

Now the cost. Olson's most recent essay, "Belle and the Meaning of Place," went to patrons in March 2026 and to the public YouTube channel on 7 July 2026 — and, as far as the public channel shows, it was the first new mainline essay there since September 2023. The patrons saw work earlier; the public waited about two and a half years. The patron-first window is the economics made visible. Once the people funding the room are the first audience, the public channel becomes a shop window for work paid for elsewhere. That is a fair trade for the patrons, and a real one for everyone else — and it is precisely why our rubric's Consistency axis, which measures what an ordinary subscriber can rely on, comes down hard on the form's best channels. Folding Ideas scores 38 there this issue; hbomberguy scores 52. We are aware that the axis effectively grades the patron economy and finds it wanting. We would answer that the rubric is a reader's instrument, not a patron's, and the reader is the one we work for.

The Audit

A form that runs on trust in the maker's research is only as strong as that research, and in December 2023 the form found out how strong. hbomberguy's "Plagiarism and You(Tube)," nearly four hours long, spent its first two hours building a case before revealing its true subject: the video essayist James Somerton, whose work the video documented as built on lifted, uncredited scholarship. Somerton shut down his accounts within a day. The video also criticised Internet Historian and Illuminaughtii over citation, and our #018 feature dealt with those cases — kept, deliberately, in proportion. We will not relitigate them here.

What matters for this feature is the economic shape of the reckoning. An economy that rewards the appearance of depth will eventually be offered the appearance without the depth: the confident voice, the long runtime, the scholarly register, assembled from other people's work. And the audit itself was a patron-shaped object. A four-hour investigation of a fellow essayist, two hours of it groundwork before the reveal, is not something an ad-revenue spreadsheet would ever greenlight. The room that made the essays possible also made the audit possible. The form had grown valuable enough to counterfeit and independent enough to police itself — two signs of a literature, arriving in the same video.

The Inversion

The most famous passage of A Room of One's Own is a thought experiment: Shakespeare's sister, Judith, born with her brother's gift and none of his access, who runs to London, is turned away from every door, and dies young with nothing written. Woolf's point was not that genius is rare. It was that genius is common and rooms are not — and that history is full of minds wasted for want of material things.

Hold that against the people this issue grades. A former teacher with a sociology background. An English-literature graduate from Halifax, West Yorkshire. A Canadian critic in Calgary. None of them were handed a commission, a column, or a studio deal. The platform did not mean to build rooms for them; its incentives pointed the other way, toward volume and frequency and the next upload. But it built the audience, and the audience built the rooms, a few dollars a month at a time. Woolf asked for money and a room so that genius could stop being wasted. Nobody granted her request on purpose. The platform granted it by accident, the strangers paid the rent, and the essays got made.

▌ DEATH OF FORMAT ▌

Game Over

Five ways the essay gets faked. The format is the corpse — never the audience. Autopsies below.

☠
The Plot-Recap "Essay"

A synopsis in a trench coat. It has the essay's posture — the serious title card, the measured voiceover, a word like "unpacking" in the thumbnail — and underneath it is a scene-by-scene retelling of a film, show, or game, read out in order, with the occasional "and this is interesting" standing in for an idea. Nothing is argued. Nothing is compared. The footage is not evidence for a claim; it is simply the thing being described, in the sequence it happened, for the length of time it takes to describe it. The format exists because summary is the cheapest script there is — the source material writes the structure for you — and because a viewer who skipped the thing, or wants to relive it, will happily sit through forty minutes of being told what happened. That audience is real and deserves better than to be sold a book report as criticism. The test is simple and brutal: remove the footage, read the script, and ask what the narrator believes. An essay has an answer. The recap has a plot, and the plot belonged to somebody else.

Retelling is not reading. If the thesis is "and then this happened," the essayist didn't watch the film — they transcribed it.
☠
The Runtime Flex

"Longer than The Irishman." It is said with pride, in comments and sometimes in the packaging, as though three and a half hours of anything were an achievement in itself. Somewhere in the past six years, length became the genre's prestige signal: the longer the essay, the more serious it must be; the bigger the timestamp in the corner of the thumbnail, the louder it announces this one is important. The best long essays earn every minute — this issue profiles essayists whose multi-hour work justifies its runtime, even where our reviews argue about who actually makes it to the end. The flex is something else. It is length as costume: sprawl mistaken for depth, a ninety-minute argument padded to three hours because three hours is what the important ones look like, digressions left in because cutting them would make the number smaller. And the platform is complicit, because watch time is the one metric it pays for without asking what filled it. A runtime is a cost the viewer pays, not a quality the essayist owns. The editor's oldest job is to make things shorter, and the flex has fired the editor.

A long essay asks for your evening. A runtime flex demands it — then spends the first hour proving it deserved the second.
☠
The Six-Minute Throat-Clear

"In this essay, I will…" — and then it doesn't. First the cold open, a dramatic clip and an ominous question. Then the title card. Then the personal preamble about how long this one took and how hard it was to make. Then a sponsor read. Then a roadmap of the chapters to come, announced like a conference keynote. Then a second sponsor. Then a thank-you to patrons. Then a third sponsor, the mobile game, for the people who stayed. Six minutes in, the thesis has been promised four times and stated zero. The format is a product of the economics we describe in Cheat Codes — integrated reads pay, patron shout-outs are owed, and an ambitious essay runs on all of it — and the money is not the crime. The crime is the order. An essay's first duty is to tell you what it thinks, so you can decide whether to give it your time; the throat-clear spends your time first and asks permission afterwards. Montaigne, who appears elsewhere in this issue, had a habit of beginning in the middle of a thought. He had no sponsors, granted, but he also had no excuse.

Say what you think, then sell the mobile game. Reverse the order and the essay becomes the ad break.
☠
The One-Lens Mill

Every video begins somewhere new — a sitcom, a cereal mascot, a racing game, a children's film — and every video arrives at precisely the same conclusion the channel has reached in every video before it. The subject changes; the destination never does. Whatever the lens is — a political framework, a psychological theory, a pet grievance — it is applied not as a tool but as a vending machine: insert any piece of culture, receive the identical verdict. Frameworks are good. Criticism needs them, and the best essayists on the platform wear theirs openly and are better for it. The mill's failure is that the framework never meets resistance. Nothing the subject shows can surprise it, complicate it, or prove it wrong, which means the subject has stopped being studied and started being used. The audience, meanwhile, is not learning to see; it is learning to recognise the answer before the video gets there, which is comfortable, and is how a readership turns into a congregation. An essay is an attempt, and an attempt you already know the result of is a ritual.

If every door opens onto the same room, the essayist isn't exploring the house. They're the house.
☠
The AI Profundity Mill

Stock nebula footage. (Not that Nebula.) A slow synthetic voice, frictionless and faintly reverent, asking whether consciousness is a simulation, whether time is an illusion, whether we are all, in a sense, stardust — over drifting galaxies, ambient pads, and the occasional rotating brain rendered in blue light. It is the essay's mood with none of its mind: awe produced industrially, questions posed and never pursued, because pursuing them would require knowing something and the pipeline does not know things. It generates. The format thrives because profundity is the one register a machine can fake convincingly at three in the morning, when the viewer is tired enough to mistake vastness for depth. The giveaway is that nothing is ever argued, cited, or concluded; the video ends where it began, with a rhetorical question and a request to subscribe "if this made you think." It didn't. It made you feel like thinking, which is a different product and a cheaper one. The video essay was the first literature written in footage. The profundity mill is the first to be written by nobody.

Wonder is a starting point, not a script. A question nobody intends to answer is just a screensaver with a voiceover.
