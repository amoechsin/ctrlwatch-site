Across nineteen issues of Boss Fights, one corner of the ring has always been the challenger. Not this time. [Jacob Geller](/reviews/jacob-geller/) holds 91 and ESSENTIAL, awarded in Issue #012. [Jenny Nicholson](/reviews/jenny-nicholson/) holds 91 and ESSENTIAL, awarded two issues later. In the [Top 50](/top50/) they sit four places apart, at #5 and #9, kept apart by a single X-Factor point and three other channels on 91. This is the first Boss Fight in the magazine's history in which both fighters walk in holding the belt, and nobody is putting it down.

Staging it at all looks like bad manners. It is the Essay Issue's whole argument. A form deserves a canon, and a canon is not a shelf of things you like. It is a running argument about which things matter more, and why, conducted in public by people who could be wrong. You don't get a canon without somebody being told, politely, that they came second. So: two ways the long essay can be great. Geller builds cathedrals. He walks in through a video game and comes out in modern art, anatomy, the haunted house and the history of fear, and his signature move is the pivot, the moment one subject turns into another without a visible seam. Nicholson builds courtrooms. She takes one subject, gives it four hours, enters every receipt into evidence and delivers it all in a deadpan so patient that it ends up reading as the verdict. One essay connects. The other convicts.

| Tale of the Tape | Jacob Geller | Jenny Nicholson |
|---|---|---|
| Method | The lyrical connector: games × art × fear × memory | The comic litigator: one subject, every receipt, total deadpan |
| Subs | Over 1.5M; essays viewed 100M+ times cumulatively (per NPR, Nov 2025) | Roughly 1.5M (at last check) |
| Output | Historically about monthly; essays typically half an hour to an hour | About one major essay a year, and each one lands as an event |
| Best known for | "Who's Afraid of Modern Art?" · "Control, Anatomy, and the Legacy of the Haunted House" | "The Spectacular Failure of the Star Wars Hotel": about four hours, twenty chapters |
| Latest | First book, *How a Game Lives* (19 Nov 2025); a second, annotated collection announced | 24 March 2026: a long-form deep dive on a Twilight knockoff |
| Weakness | Cadence slowed around the books; a register that can tip into the sermon | One major essay a year; the method doesn't scale, and was never meant to |

### Round 1 — Content Quality

Each of them has a method that nobody else on the platform executes at this level. Geller's craft is the join you can't see. "Control, Anatomy, and the Legacy of the Haunted House" starts inside a video game and ends up somewhere architectural and bodily, and you never catch the moment it changed subjects. At full power it is the best prose being spoken aloud on YouTube. When it overreaches, the register lifts off into the sermon, and you can hear a thought being performed instead of thought.

Nicholson's craft is accumulation. "The Spectacular Failure of the Star Wars Hotel" runs about four hours across twenty chapters on a Disney hotel that opened in March 2022 and closed roughly eighteen months later. (Our #014 review said twenty months. We were wrong, and she would have caught it.) Her jokes double as evidence. She describes a sincere, enormous, expensive thing so calmly and precisely that the description becomes the joke and then becomes the case. Her risk runs the other way from his: a method built on completeness can start treating completeness as its own justification. Both score 96, one a lyric and the other a deposition. Choosing between them would be grading genres, not essays. **Draw. The cathedral and the courtroom, both built to code.**

### Round 2 — Consistency

This is the least glamorous round, and the only one neither fighter would choose to be judged on. Nicholson works to a roughly annual calendar of major essays. The latest verified one, a deep dive on a Twilight knockoff, landed on 24 March 2026 the way her big work always lands: as an event, and long. Geller's rhythm has historically run closer to monthly, though it has thinned while the books took the oxygen. *How a Game Lives* came out on 19 November 2025, and a second collection, *You're Not Overthinking It: The Annotated Essays of Jacob Geller*, has been announced. Ink is a real competitor for an essayist's year, and we won't pretend otherwise. Consistency measures only one thing, though: whether a subscriber can expect to hear from you. On any ordinary year, a Geller subscriber hears from him more often than a Nicholson subscriber hears from her. It's three points, 68 to 65, and the scorecard has them right. **Geller wins, by the width of a calendar page.**

### Round 3 — Replay Value

Both score 95, and the matching numbers hide two completely different machines. Geller's archive is a web. "Returnal is a Hell of Our Own Creation" reads differently once you've sat with "Every Zelda is the Darkest Zelda", because the catalogue keeps returning to the same questions from new doors, and every new thread pulls on the old ones. *How a Game Lives* makes the argument for us: ten essays judged worth re-examining in print with new commentary is a replay-value case put by a publisher.

Nicholson's catalogue replays like a building you know. A four-hour essay is somewhere you go back to, and the second visit shows you the architecture: the callbacks, the slow escalation, the evidence you laughed past the first time that was doing structural work all along. His essays reward rereading the way poetry does. Hers reward it the way a great trial transcript does. Neither deserves a point less. **Draw. Nothing in either archive is expiring.**

### Round 4 — Community

The scorecard says 90 each. Those numbers were set two issues apart, and in a re-affirmation fight we don't touch them. Head to head, though, this round has a clear owner. Geller's audience is a seminar. His comment sections are where viewers trade books, paintings and counter-readings and carry his arguments into places he never went, which is exactly the community a connector should have.

Nicholson's audience is rarer: a mass public that has agreed to wait. NPR called the hotel essay "this summer's must-see documentary", and the summer went along with it. A four-hour video about a closed Disney hotel spread to millions of viewers, which means millions of people gave up an evening to hear a woman read them the evidence. She has built trust at stadium scale and kept it at kitchen-table temperature. She talks to the audience like a friend who has been somewhere ridiculous and owes you the full account, and they behave like friends: they wait a year and they turn up. Geller has built the best reading group on the platform. Nicholson has built a standing appointment, and in 2026 that is the harder thing to keep. **Nicholson wins, outright.**

### Round 5 — X-Factor

One round each and two drawn, so the fight goes where the table already went: the axis where they are separated by a single point, 97 to 96. That tie-break is why Geller sits at #5 and Nicholson at #9. The table settled it with arithmetic. The ring has to settle it with an argument, and both cases are strong.

Nicholson's is the case from the age of slop. The cheapest thing on YouTube in 2026 is a confident narrator. The AI profundity mill will produce one on demand, by the yard, over stock nebula footage. What nobody can fake is four sourced hours: the trip actually taken, the twenty chapters assembled by someone who was there and checked. Her method proves a human was present, and the closer the platform drifts toward synthetic, the more that proof is worth.

Geller's is the case from the age of fragmentation. The platform is now built out of slices, with clips and Shorts and a recommendation engine that seals every interest into its own room. Geller's entire method refuses the sealed room. He takes a video game, a modern painting and the history of the haunted house and argues, in what feels like one unbroken sentence, that they were always a single subject. This is the [Type 7 Collision](/concepts/collision-channels/) (DepthCharge's term) at its apex. The mills can imitate his tone. They can't imitate the connection, because the connection is a mind arriving somewhere, and a style sheet can't get you there.

So which kind of irreplaceability does the essay itself reward? Go back to the word. *Essai* means an attempt: a mind trying itself against a subject and following the thinking wherever it leads. Nicholson's method is the finest instrument the form has, with its rigour, its structure and its deadpan run as cross-examination. Geller's method is what the form is: a mind visibly moving from one thing to the thing it turns out to be about. Both are irreplaceable, but only one of them in the form's native sense. By one point, and reluctantly, the round goes to the cathedral. **Geller wins the round by a single point, and with it the fight.**

### The Decision

Jacob Geller wins. The overall doesn't decide it, because the scorecard reads 91 to 91, and the round count only half does: he takes two, she takes one, and two are drawn. He wins because the essay form casts the deciding vote on the axis this magazine weights heaviest. Strip the long essay down to the one thing it can't do without and you're left with a mind making connections nobody else would make. Geller does that more purely than anyone currently working.

The more important point is what Jenny Nicholson takes out of this ring. Every year the platform gets better at producing things shaped like essays: the runtime flex, the plot recap in a trench coat, the synthetic awe. Against all of that, a four-hour, twenty-chapter, fully sourced account of something a person actually went and did is the one product the mills can't counterfeit. If the video essay gets through this decade with its reputation intact, part of the reason will be that she made the real thing impossible to confuse with the fake. Geller shows what the form is for. Nicholson is the reason anyone will still trust it.

> Jenny Nicholson is the essay's best defence. Jacob Geller is its definition. Only one of those wins a Boss Fight; the form needs both to survive the decade.

And so the twentieth issue argues with itself, which is what a canon is for. We could be wrong about this. A canon that couldn't be wrong would just be a playlist.
