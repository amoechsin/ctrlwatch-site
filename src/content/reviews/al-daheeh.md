Count the addresses. Around 2014, a young Egyptian starts an explainer show on his own YouTube channel. In 2017 it signs with AJ+, Al Jazeera's digital arm, where it reportedly gains millions of subscribers and hundreds of millions of views. In June 2020 AJ+ ends the contract. By late 2020 the show has a new home at the UAE's New Media Academy and a new format, "The Daheeh Museum," with deals carrying it to Starzplay, MBC Shahid and, around March 2025, a Spacetoon cartoon series. That run ends after nearly five years, the show citing more than 4.7 billion cumulative views across platforms — its own figure, printed here as its own. And in April 2026, Al Jazeera 360 and AJ+ announce a new season. Three moves, two paymasters who agree on very little, one voice.

Al-Daheeh is CTRL+WATCH's first Arabic-language Player Profile, the overdue answer to the Arabic-language gap the magazine flagged in Issue #013, and it is the right first one, because few channels make the essay's quietest claim more forcefully: the platform is never the point. The voice is. Its audience has followed it through every door.

One disclosure, made once and plainly: our seat for this review is further from the stage than usual. We could not verify individual episode titles to the standard this magazine now holds itself to, so this profile works at the level of arc, register and cultural position, and it names no episode it cannot stand behind. Readers who watch the show in its own language will see things we can't. We would rather print that than fake the view.

#### What It Does

The host is Ahmed El-Ghandour, born in Mansoura in 1994, with a biology degree from the American University in Cairo and a master's in education from the University of Hong Kong — which is to say the CV is the show: a scientist who went and studied how people learn. The format is rapid-fire edutainment across science, history and culture, delivered in Egyptian Arabic at machine-gun pace, and the register is unmistakable even from where we're sitting: the fast-talking cousin who has read everything and cannot wait to tell you. Its footprint is documented well beyond its own channels. The Tunisian outlet Kapitalis called El-Ghandour "the most influential YouTuber in the Arab world"; the 1 Billion Followers Summit ran a workshop on the show's method, reported by the UAE's state news agency WAM; Dar El Shorouk published a behind-the-scenes book; Ahram covered a football spin-off trending on YouTube.

#### What It Does Extraordinarily Well

The X-Factor at 92 is that register, and its significance is cultural before it is comic. Serious subjects in Arabic media have traditionally arrived in the formal register; Al-Daheeh delivers them in the language people actually speak at home, at the speed people actually talk when they're excited, and the effect is to move science from the lectern to the living room. The Community at 90 is the audience that made every move with it — through three platform changes, a format reinvention and a switch of paymasters, it kept turning up.

> Al-Daheeh has changed paymasters more often than most channels change thumbnail fonts, and its audience walked through every door behind it. The only thing in the whole arc that never moved was the voice.

The strangest evidence for that Community score is the ugliest chapter in the show's history. In June 2021, after the move to the UAE-backed New Media Academy, a boycott campaign spread under the slogan قاطع الدحيح — "boycott Daheeh" — with critics tying the new platform to the regional politics of normalisation with Israel. El-Ghandour answered the criticism publicly; Masrawy reported his response that month. The evenhanded reading, and the one we hold, is that this was the cost of platform churn rather than a verdict on the man: across its life, the show's funding homes have been geopolitical actors on more than one side of the region's arguments, Doha's Al Jazeera on one hand and a UAE-backed academy on the other. An Arabic education show at this scale has, so far, only ever found homes that were also somebody's soft power. And nobody organises a boycott of a show they don't care about.

#### Where It Falls Short

The weaknesses follow the addresses. Consistency at 62 measures the house, not the effort: every contract ending is an interruption, every new home a reset, and the archive is scattered across channels and services in a way that makes the show's own history hard to hold in one place. Even the subscriber figures conflict depending on which era's channel you count, which is why we print none. Replay Value at 78 is the price of topical, high-velocity episodes in a scattered archive: the work rewards a return, if you can find where it now lives. And Content Quality at 84 is the axis our distance constrains most. We can price the format, the reputation and the reach with confidence; we cannot grade episode by episode, and the number is deliberately conservative because of it.

**The verdict.** EXCELLENT, 81, and no [Top 50](/top50/) seat at a threshold of 85 — but the score is not the headline. The column is.
