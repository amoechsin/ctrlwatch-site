This is the Maker Issue. Every page of it is about people who build things: a woman who converted a Tesla into a truck, a man who has spent ten years on one instrument, five dead engineers summoned to look at the workshop the internet became. And so it is the right issue — the only issue, really — in which to review the largest "craft" channel most children on this platform will ever see, and to say the thing plainly: nothing is ever made here. Not once, not really. That is the entire review. The rest is evidence.

#### What It Is

The scale first, because the scale is the story. Troom Troom's main English channel stands at roughly 23.8 million subscribers and 10.6 billion views by vidIQ's October 2026 figures. Around it sits a constellation of language clones that would be the envy of any broadcaster: an Arabic channel tracked at about 12.5 million subscribers, an Indian channel at about 13.1 million, plus French, Turkish, German, Italian, Japanese and more, each a mirror of the same format — brightly lit hands, "hacks," pranks, crafts, a narrator whose cadence never quite lands on human. The figures are third-party tracker snapshots from 2025–26 and we flag them as such; their collective shape is not in doubt. This is one of the biggest craft operations in the history of moving pictures.

And here is what the written record contains about who runs it: almost nothing. The channel dates to the mid-2010s and is widely reported to have originated in Odesa; beyond that, the ownership is unconfirmed, and even Wikipedia's entry leads with how little is known about its origins. We searched. A channel with the audience of a mid-sized country is, for all practical purposes, anonymous. Compare that with every other channel in this issue, where a human being signs the work — and with 5-Minute Crafts, the genre's other giant, whose owner TheSoul at least holds press days. (They are, for the record, separate operations; this is not that review, and #004C already exists.)

#### What the 88 Is For

The negative-review protocol of this magazine requires us to name what a channel does well, genuinely, and here it is: the factory never misses. Consistency 88 — the machine ships, in a dozen languages, on a cadence most human creators couldn't survive a month of. Somewhere behind the anonymity is a production operation of real industrial competence: casting, sets, scripting, localisation, thumbnails, running for a decade at planetary scale. That is the one real thing. And it is exactly what makes the rest worse, because competence this complete leaves no room for the usual excuses. Nothing here is accidental. The craft that doesn't work is not a failure of craft. It is the absence of craft, wearing its costume, manufactured deliberately and at scale.

#### Where It Fails Its Audience

Because here is what the competence produces: "hacks" performed rather than invented, crafts that exist from camera-on to camera-off, solutions to problems no one has, demonstrated by actors and addressed — overwhelmingly, unmistakably — to children. The press that bothered to look reached remarkably consistent conclusions: the New Statesman in 2018 found the videos "unnerving, absurd, and surreal" and asked who was behind them; Vox filed the genre under bizarre DIY videos and asked why YouTube was riddled with them; the French trade press described, in so many words, a content farm for children. We will add one finding of our own, and it is the most damning sentence we can write about a channel this size: in preparing this review, we could not find a single Troom Troom video title in the written record worth quoting — not one upload that any critic, anywhere, found worth naming. Ten billion views and no titles. The output is weather, not work.

Run the axes: Content Quality 22 — production competence cannot rescue content whose premise is that making things is a prop. Replay Value 15 — nothing here is built to be seen twice; it is built to be next. Community 35 — an audience of children at a scale no one audits, around work that never answers back. X-Factor 18 — the format is the anti-signature: the entire operation is engineered to have no author at all. The maker video, whatever its genre, has one irreducible promise: what you are watching was actually done, by someone, who put their name on it. Troom Troom inverts every clause.

**The verdict.** GAME OVER, 31 — beneath [Ridddle's](/reviews/ridddle/) 38, above [Bright Side's](/reviews/bright-side/) 28, in the wing of the archive reserved for channels that are not bad at the thing but are instead a factory for its absence. The mandatory negative this cycle was always going to live in this issue, because the Maker Issue has a thesis — that building things in public, failures included, is the most honest genre YouTube has — and Troom Troom is its exact negation: a craft channel, the biggest one there is, on which nothing has ever been made.
