In March 2016, a Swedish musician uploaded a video of a hand-cranked wooden machine playing a song with two thousand marbles. The marbles fell through gates onto a vibraphone, a bass, a drum kit; the machine wheezed and clattered and very nearly didn't make it to the end of the tune; and the clip went around the world — hundreds of millions of cumulative views by the figures that circulated in its Wikipedia era, numbers we print loosely because they long ago stopped mattering. The Marble Machine is one of YouTube's canonical videos, full stop. What matters to this review is what Martin Molin did next, because it is the strangest career decision on this platform: he has spent the decade since building its successor, in public, without finishing.

#### What It Does

Understand the shape of the thing. The original Marble Machine was retired almost immediately — it barely survived its own video. From January 2017, in a weekly series the channel ran as "Wintergatan Wednesdays," Molin built the Marble Machine X: a precision-engineered successor meant to tour. Four years in, at the end of 2021, he stopped — the design, by his own public accounting, would never be robust enough for the road — and in March 2022 he began designing Marble Machine 3 from scratch, livestreaming the CAD. The abandoned MMX is bound for a German mechanical-music museum as a living exhibit. MM3 is meant to be everything X wasn't: portable, plywood, human-powered. The cadence now runs a video every three to six weeks, as best we can establish, each one documenting gears, marble dividers, and the slow accumulation of a machine that does not yet play concerts.

That is the whole channel. One instrument, unfinished, for ten years. There is nothing else like it on YouTube, and the Maker Issue exists partly to ask what to do with it.

#### What It Does Extraordinarily Well

The Community score of 92 is the highest in this issue's Player Profiles, and it is the entire economic story: Wintergatan is funded by Patreon and memberships — by the audience, directly, with no number printed here because we couldn't verify one — which means thousands of people pay monthly for a machine that may never be finished, and have done so across a full redesign-from-zero. That is not an audience. That is a congregation. They watched four years of work get honourably abandoned at the end of 2021 and responded by funding the next attempt. No sponsor would have survived that meeting; the patrons didn't blink.

Content Quality at 84 prices genuine engineering content wrapped in a musician's sensibility — Molin is an ex-member of a touring band, and the machine exists to serve a groove, which keeps every engineering decision tethered to a musical consequence. And the X-Factor at 89 is the honesty. When MMX died, it died on camera, with its builder explaining exactly why. Our Cheat Codes feature this issue argues that failure is the maker genre's native fuel; Wintergatan is the channel that runs on nothing else.

#### Where It Falls Short

Now the critique, which is real and we will not soften it: at some point the cycle of redesign became the product, and the channel has never answered the question of whether it can survive its own success condition. A decade in, the sequence runs machine, abandoned machine, machine — and a viewer who joined for the 2016 video has watched two complete acts of not-finishing. Replay Value at 80 reflects an archive that is genuinely fascinating and almost impossible to enter midstream; Consistency at 55 prices a weekly series that ended with the MMX era and a current rhythm that breathes with the build. The uncomfortable question underneath the whole enterprise: if MM3 is ever finished, toured, and filmed, what is the channel the morning after? We suspect Molin has asked himself the same thing, somewhere around gear three hundred.

**The verdict.** EXCELLENT, 82, and no [Top 50](/top50/) seat at a threshold of 85 — scored below [Simone Giertz](/reviews/simone-giertz/), who ships, and [Stuff Made Here](/reviews/stuff-made-here/), who finishes, and above almost everyone for the purity of the attempt. [Clickspring](/reviews/clickspring/) holds #22 for machining a clock across years of patient uploads; Wintergatan is the shadow version of the same virtue, patience without an endpoint. Somewhere in a Swedish workshop there is a plywood wheel that explains the entire creator economy: it turns because people pay to watch it turn, and if it ever plays the song, everyone — builder, patrons, magazine — will have to decide what they were actually subscribed to.
