In November 2019, within hours of The Athletic's report on the Houston Astros, a Yankees fan with editing software posted a video that did what the sport's entire credentialed press corps had not: it showed you the cheating. Bang. Bang. Jimmy O'Brien synced the dugout trash-can audio to the pitches, on screen, checkably, and the sign-stealing scandal stopped being an allegation and became a thing you had heard with your own ears. ESPN later credited the internet with helping crack the case; the internet, in this instance, was one guy. That video is the channel's founding myth and also its thesis: modern sport generates more evidence than its institutions are willing to look at, and a stranger with patience and a timeline can be the most trusted analyst in the room.

#### What It Does

The signature format is the breakdown — and the lip-read is the invention. An umpire ejects a manager; the broadcast gives you thirty seconds of distant shouting; Jomboy gives you the screenplay, reconstructed word by word from the mouths, annotated, paced like a two-hander. It is equal parts forensics and comedy, and it created a genre now imitated across every sport on the platform. Around the format, O'Brien and co-founder Jake Storiale have built an actual media company since 2017 — Talkin' Baseball, Talkin' Yanks, The Warehouse Games, a bench of shows deep enough that the Consistency 96 is simply a description of the machine. Baseball happens daily; so does Jomboy.

And in 2025 came the plot twist that makes this a Competition Issue story: Major League Baseball itself took an equity stake in the company — by most accounts the league's first ownership investment in a creator operation, after a negotiation O'Brien has said ran four years and started from terms he called a non-starter. The league that got caught by a breakdown video decided the correct long-term response was to own a piece of the desk.

#### What It Does Extraordinarily Well

Two things, and they are rarer together than apart. First, the forensics: the Astros file, the ump-show taxonomy, a decade of receipts assembled with genuine editorial patience — slowed footage, synced audio, the claim always checkable on screen. Second, the warmth. The breakdowns never curdle into gotcha content, because underneath the forensics is a fan's delight in the sport's pettiest theatre: a balk dispute treated as grand human drama, a bat-flip feud narrated like Shakespeare in miniature. That combination is the X-Factor at 92 — a voice the entire genre now orbits, and a tone dozens of imitators copy without ever quite landing.

> Jomboy proved the scoreboard was lying before the league did. The league's answer, six years later, was to buy shares in the guy who checks.

#### Where It Falls Short

The record isn't spotless, and we grade the record. The "buzzer" theory the channel amplified in the Astros scandal's aftermath was never substantiated — a reminder that forensic confidence and forensic proof are different products. This summer a Dodgers prospect pushed back publicly on being breakdown fodder, a fair glimpse of the format's unpriced cost: the people in the clips never volunteered. And the MLB stake cuts both ways forever now. Independence was the product; the shareholder register says otherwise; every future controversy touching the league will be read through it, and should be. Replay Value at 70 is the other honest number — breakdowns are news, and news expires. Nobody rewatches a 2021 ejection breakdown except as trivia.

**The verdict.** EXCELLENT, 85, and into the [Top 50](/top50/) at #50, where this issue's arithmetic was unusually bloody. The lip-read is a genuine invention, the Astros work is a permanent entry in the platform's journalism ledger, and the question the equity deal opens — what happens the first time the desk's biggest story costs its shareholder — is one we intend to be watching when it arrives.
