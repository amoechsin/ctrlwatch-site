Every so often the non-English column of this magazine gets to file a report the Anglosphere genuinely needs, and this is one: the most important competition organiser on the modern internet is not a league, a federation, or a platform. He is a thirty-one-year-old from Bilbao who started as a League of Legends caster in Spain's LVP in 2014 — best caster in the league four years running — went independent in early 2021, and has since invented more successful competitive formats from scratch than the entire franchised-esports industry managed in a decade of burning venture capital.

#### What It Does

La Velada del Año is the flagship: an amateur boxing card where streamers and creators train for months and fight in front of actual stadiums. In five editions it grew from a Barcelona club night in 2021 to the single biggest livestream any individual channel has ever produced — the 2025 edition peaked above nine million concurrent viewers, a record for the entire medium, and the 2026 edition simulcast across Twitch, YouTube, and TikTok to a reported thirty-plus million devices. There are World Cup broadcasts that would take those numbers. The Kings League, co-founded in 2022 with Gerard Piqué, took seven-a-side football and rebuilt it with game-design logic — secret weapons, penalty roulettes, streamer-owned teams broadcasting their own matches — and is expanding across the Americas with nine-figure ambitions. The Balloon World Cup — keeping a balloon off the floor, as an international televised sport — was a one-joke format that half a million people watched sincerely.

The pattern is the point. Where esports tried to imitate the NBA and died of the overheads, Ibai starts from the other end: what would be joyful to watch? What can a community feel it owns? The competition is designed as content from the first whistle — and that turns out not to be a corruption of sport but a return to what sport was before television dressed it in a league office.

#### What It Does Extraordinarily Well

Community 93 and X-Factor 96 are as high as this magazine has scored either axis for a non-English creator, because the intangible here is genuinely singular: Ibai is the proof, at civilisational scale, that one person with a community can out-organise an industry. The events work because the belonging predates the bell — the fighters are people the audience already has years of parasocial history with, the stakes are legible without a rulebook, and the broadcast belongs to the people in it. The esports winter our Issue #019 feature autopsies is, in miniature, the story of everyone who got that order backwards.

> The esports winter froze every league that tried to become television. Ibai never tried — he became a carnival instead, and carnivals don't have winters.

#### Where It Falls Short

Platform honesty first: this is a YouTube magazine and Ibai is not primarily a YouTube phenomenon. He is the most-followed channel on Twitch on Earth; YouTube — sixteen to seventeen million subscribers, depending which tracker you squint at — is his archive and his overflow car park. The channel's daily output is streaming culture's: reactions, food tours, event VODs, a torrent of Consistency-88 material with the shelf life of fruit. Replay Value 58 is the price of living live; almost nothing here is built to be watched twice, La Velada excepted. The occasional flare-up — a botched chilaquiles that annoyed Mexico, a World Cup prediction that annoyed it again — is the cost of talking on camera eight hours a day, and none of it rises above weather.

**The verdict.** EXCELLENT, 81. The number will look low to his fans and high to anyone who only counts YouTube, and both readings miss the exercise: on this platform's terms, a channel that is majority archive cannot argue past a [Top 50](/top50/) that now cuts at 85. The standing non-English commitment — Nota Bene in #018, Dot CSV in #017 — continues here, and continues because of creators like this, not as a quota.
